About

Stéphane Cuenat, Principal Software Architect in the Swiss Jura with a PhD in computer science, writes essays and working papers on what AI really changes, and what it doesn’t.

I’m Stéphane Cuenat, based in Porrentruy, in the Swiss Jura. By day I’m Principal Software Architect at Mastercam, where I work on Mastercam Copilot, bringing AI into CAM software. Before that I led development and research teams, and early in my career I had the chance to take part in SwissCube, Switzerland’s first satellite.

I hold a PhD in computer science from FEMTO-ST and the Université Marie et Louis Pasteur (formerly Université de Franche-Comté). My research combined deep learning and digital holography to measure position in 3D with sub-micrometer accuracy, using small transformer networks fast enough to run on a CPU. I still write research papers with FEMTO-ST, especially with Raphaël Couturier. The papers, the thesis and the data are listed on my Research page.

This site is where I think in public. I write about what AI really changes and what it doesn’t: learning and the time it takes, why we need less data rather than more, what machines can and can’t rebuild, and what wisdom means next to intelligence. Lately that has taken four directions: what agents change in building software and in the teams around it, why small, fast, local models can do more than we expect, what a dashboard should really show, and, some evenings, physics. Some pieces are short essays from my newsletter AI, Honestly. Others are longer working papers, often with the code and data to check them.

Start here

  • Agentic Coding. A series in five steps: what agents decide, how they reach other systems, why intent becomes the source, how to review it, and why routine code review should give way to a thin gate at the point of no return. Its follow-up, Work Has Mass, looks at the team around the code: why Scrum and SAFe should get lighter, not heavier.
  • Show What Changed, Not the Data. The working paper behind much of this site: a dashboard should show how our understanding changed, not the data. Then What a Quiet Screen Cannot See, which answers a reader’s objection to it.
  • Fast AI. Learn slowly, once; act fast, every time. Its companion, An Ecosystem, Not an Oracle, argues that local AI will be many small specialists sharing a memory, and Where Memory Lives puts that memory to the test in four experiments.
  • A Universe Without a Bottom. Something different: a personal detour into physics. Its follow-up, Decided in the Bulk, Written on the Boundary, turns the question into an experiment on AI image generators, and Nothing From Outside, Everything From Inside is a second evening of curiosity, followed by a third, What Survives Comparison.
  • My TEDx talk. The talk, and The Answer You Can’t Check, the essay I wrote alongside it.

Or browse all the essays.

I’m interested in ideas more than hype, so challenges and counter-arguments are always welcome.

How I write. The questions, ideas and arguments here are mine, and so is the responsibility for them. But I don’t write alone: I use AI to challenge my ideas, shape them, tighten the text and help with code and experiments. It would be odd to write about AI honestly and hide that.
