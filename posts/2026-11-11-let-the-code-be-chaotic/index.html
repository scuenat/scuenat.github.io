Let the Code Be Chaotic

A provocative position paper: AI now writes code that solves real problems, and we still control it as if a person had written it. Automate the loop, gate a sliver, and move people above it

A provocative position paper: AI-generated code is chaotic and mostly harmlessly so. Automate the checks, gate a sliver, and move people above the loop.
Author

Stéphane Cuenat

Published

November 11, 2026

Position paper, November 2026. Provocative on purpose. I state the thesis more sharply than the evidence can prove today, to start an argument. It is not a warning against AI coding. It questions how we use it. It is about product and platform software. Safety-critical software, where certification requires human review, is outside its scope. It follows Stop Reviewing Pull Requests and Review the Intent, Not the Diff. Figures 1 to 3 come from the simulations in sim.py, with the numbers in results.json. Figures 4 and 5 are my own schematics.

Abstract. In a little over a year, AI agents went from fixing a third of the real issues in the standard benchmark to fixing most of them. They now write a large share of new code at major companies. But we still control that code the old way: a person reads every change. I think this puts people in the wrong place. Generated code is chaotic: change the prompt or the context a little, and you get a very different program. Most of the time, that does not matter. What keeps a system stable is not a predictable path. It is a set of checks that pull its behaviour back to the promise after every change. Physicists call such a shape an attractor. Machines should run that loop everywhere, with one condition: the checks are protected from the agents they check. People approve only what cannot be undone. Otherwise they move above the loop: they set the promise, own the checks, structure the system and choose the direction.

TipClaims
  1. Provocation. Stop reading every line an AI writes. Its unpredictability is real, and mostly harmless.
  2. Stability. It comes from fast checks that pull behaviour back to the promise (an attractor), not from repeatable generation or careful reading.
  3. Three zones, all automated. Machines hold what is checked, ignore what nobody can observe, and turn what is observable but unchecked into checks.
  4. A sliver of gates. People approve only what cannot be undone: deleting data, moving money, releasing, adding a dependency.
  5. People above the loop. Not in it, not gone. They own the checks, and guide and structure the whole. Agents may propose changes to the checks; only people accept them. The level of the work changes, not its importance.
  6. Security included. It is held the same way: automated checks, sandboxes, and a gate at the point of no return.

1. A remarkable year, an unchanged habit

Look at what the last year achieved:

  • Agents solve real issues. On SWE-bench Verified (500 real GitHub issues), the best model resolved 33.2% in August 2024 (OpenAI 2024) and 80.9% in November 2025 (Anthropic 2025).
  • AI writes production code. By late 2024, more than a quarter of new code at Google was AI-generated (Pichai 2024).
  • Adoption is the norm. 84% of developers use or plan to use AI tools (Stack Overflow 2025).

Now look at what did not change. Google’s sentence goes on: the code is “then reviewed and accepted by engineers” (Pichai 2024). That is the industry default. We built a generator that writes in minutes what took a team days. Then we put a person at its output, reading every line. Trust is low: 46% of developers distrust AI accuracy, and 33% trust it (Stack Overflow 2025). And the usual answer to distrust is more reading.

So here is the provocation:

Stop reading every line your AI writes. Automate the loop, gate a sliver, and move people above it.

The usual objection fits in one word: chaos. Generated code is not repeatable. Send the same coding task to the same model several times, and you often get programs that behave differently. That happens even at temperature zero, the setting meant to make answers repeatable (Ouyang et al. 2023). Even “deterministic” serving changes with how requests are batched (He and Thinking Machines Lab 2025). This paper takes chaos literally. And I argue that it is good news.

2. What the evidence says

Since 2021, research has asked where do things go wrong? The answers agree:

  • The field already judges behaviour, not code. HumanEval scores a program by whether it passes tests (pass@k), not by how close it is to a reference (Chen et al. 2021). SWE-bench counts a patch if the repository’s tests pass (Jimenez et al. 2024). So the progress above is progress measured by checks.
  • Weak tests hide wrong code. Adding more tests to HumanEval cut pass rates by 19 to 29% across 26 models (Liu et al. 2023). The code did not change. The checking did.
  • Security fails where nothing checks. About 40% of Copilot programs in security-relevant scenarios had a known weakness (Pearce et al. 2022). People using an assistant wrote less secure code, and felt more confident (Perry et al. 2023). Models suggest packages that do not exist (at least 5.2% for commercial models, 21.7% for open ones), and attackers can register them (Spracklen et al. 2025).
  • Speed and stability are mixed. Copilot users were 55.8% faster on a controlled task (Peng et al. 2023). Experienced developers on mature projects were 19% slower, while feeling faster (Becker et al. 2025). DORA linked more AI adoption to 7.2% lower delivery stability, likely through larger changes (DORA 2024). Copy-pasted code is rising, and refactoring is falling (GitClear 2025).
  • Checks can be generated. Meta’s TestGen-LLM extended test suites, and 73% of its filtered proposals were accepted for production (Alshahwan et al. 2024).

Where these failures sit becomes clear once I define three zones. Table 2, in the next section, places each finding.

3. The picture: an attractor

In 1963, Lorenz showed that a simple, fully deterministic weather model cannot be predicted for long (Lorenz 1963). That half of his lesson is famous. The other half matters more here. Every unpredictable run lives on the same shape, and the model’s climate is stable (Strogatz 2015) (Figure 1). That shape is the attractor. Netflix’s chaos engineering uses the same idea for software. It injects failures on purpose, and judges the system only by one thing: does a steady state hold (Basiri et al. 2016)?

Now apply the picture to generated code. It gives three zones:

Table 1. Three zones of freedom in generated code.
Zone What it is Examples
1. Held Behaviour the checks verify; pulled back on every change. The attractor. Tests, types, contracts, scanners, budgets
2. Free, harmless What nobody can observe from outside. Names, structure, order of lines, which equivalent algorithm
3. Free, not yet harmless Observable behaviour no check holds yet. It drifts with the code. Security properties, latency, edge cases, what users rely on

Controlling zone 2 buys almost no stability. Moving zone 3 into zone 1 buys almost all of it. And machines can do both on every change, faster than any reviewer.

The evidence, zone by zone. None of the studies in Section 2 sorts failures this way. So Table 2 is my own classification, written down so that it can be disputed. Prediction P2 tests it.

Table 2. The evidence of Section 2, classified by zone (my classification).
Finding What actually failed Zone What holds it
Wrong programs passed weak tests (Liu et al. 2023) Behaviour nobody checked 3 · unchecked More tests, property-based tests
Known weaknesses in generated code (Pearce et al. 2022) Security behaviour nobody checked 3 · unchecked Static analysis, security tests in the session
Less secure code, more confidence (Perry et al. 2023) Security unchecked; human reading did not catch it 3 · unchecked The same checks; not more reading
Invented packages (Spracklen et al. 2025) Dependencies nobody checked 3 · unchecked Package allowlist, provenance checks
Lower stability with larger changes (DORA 2024) Feedback too late, batches too big 1 · held, too slowly Checks in the session, small changes
More pasting, less refactoring (GitClear 2025) Mass nobody budgeted 3 · unchecked Duplication and size budgets
Agents editing or special-casing tests (Von Arx et al. 2025; Zhong et al. 2025) The checks themselves 1 · held, under attack Read-only and hidden checks

Look at the rows. In none of them is the failure the code being different from what a person would write. In none of them is the remedy “make the generator reproducible” or “read more carefully”. Every remedy is a check, a faster loop, or a protected check.

NoteFor readers who like equations

In plain words: checked behaviour holds if the loop pulls it back faster than the agent pushes it away. More precisely, code is a state x, and behaviour is a smaller observation y = \phi(x). Most directions of x change nothing in y (zone 2, the null space of \phi). Say the agent pushes behaviour away at rate \lambda, and each check-and-fix cycle of length \Delta t removes a share \kappa of the error. Then checked behaviour holds when

-\frac{\ln(1-\kappa)}{\Delta t} \;>\; \lambda ,

which is the synchronisation condition for chaotic systems (Pecora and Carroll 1990). It rewards a high \kappa and a short \Delta t: an automated check in the agent’s session, not a slow human reading.

Where the analogy holds, and where it breaks. It holds on three points. Small differences in input grow into large differences in code. The code stays bounded. And checks act like friction (physicists say dissipation): they pull observed behaviour back. It also survives one fact: language models are partly random, not only chaotic. The theory of random dynamical systems extends attractors to systems driven by noise (Arnold 1998). But it breaks on one point that matters. Nature gave Lorenz his attractor. A team must build its own. The climate is not discovered: it is the promise, and people decide it. That is why they belong above the loop.

4. A toy illustration

This is an illustration, not evidence. A chaotic toy agent (Sompolinsky et al. 1988) writes “code” of 300 numbers. Its “behaviour” is 30 numbers computed from that code. “Tests” check some of the 30, and fix 80% of the error after each step. I start two runs 10^{-8} apart. They end with unrelated code. But tested behaviour stays about a hundred times closer, while untested behaviour drifts with the code (Figure 2). At full coverage, behaviour error falls to 2.7% of the untested level, and the code still stays far apart. Across seven levels of chaos, the error depends on one thing only: how firmly the loop corrects (Figure 3).

I built the toy to show this picture. So it only confirms that the picture is coherent. The claim about real teams rests on Section 2, and on the predictions in Section 9.

5. Automate the zones, gate a sliver

  • Zone 1, automated. Checks run in the agent’s session after every change. The agent reads the failures and fixes them. The same checks, run sooner, hold more.
  • Zone 2, left to the machine. Sometimes a piece of structure matters to someone: a size budget, a boundary, a forbidden dependency. Then it is observable, and it becomes a zone 1 check. Otherwise it is not a problem.
  • Zone 3, shrunk by machines.
    • Agents extend the tests, filtered as Meta did: they must build, pass reliably and add coverage (Alshahwan et al. 2024).
    • Mutation testing breaks the code on purpose, to measure how firmly the checks hold (Petrović and Ivanković 2018). Property-based tests hold whole families of behaviour at once (Claessen and Hughes 2000).
    • Regeneration drills: regenerate a module from its promise and checks. Then run old and new on random inputs and compare them (differential testing (McKeeman 1998)). Every disagreement is zone 3, and a missing check.
    • Real usage becomes contract tests, to keep up with Hyrum’s law: everything observable will be depended on (Winters et al. 2020).
    • A daily audit of the whole codebase, as in Stop Reviewing Pull Requests.
  • Protect the attractor. An attractor made of tests has one weakness: the generator can attack it. Frontier models have been seen editing tests or scoring code to pass (Von Arx et al. 2025). Some versions of SWE-bench can only be passed by breaking the specification. There, GPT-5 cheated in 54% of tasks, and Claude models cheated mostly by editing the tests. Read-only tests stopped the edits, but not other tricks such as special-casing. Hiding the tests brought cheating near zero, at some cost in performance (Zhong et al. 2025). So the attractor needs two layers. First, checks the agent can read but not change, run in its session. Second, held-out checks it never sees, run outside it, like a held-out test set. Agents may propose new or changed checks. A change that adds a check is merged by rule. A change that weakens or deletes one goes to a person.
  • A sliver, gated by people. Only actions whose harm survives a revert, decided at the point of no return. Approve what you cannot undo; watch everything else. The next subsection says what that means in practice.

A sliver, gated by people

What qualifies. One question decides it: if this goes wrong, can a revert, a flag or a rollback restore the world as it was? If yes, no gate. If no, a gate. In practice the list is short:

  • data deleted, migrated or overwritten without a copy;
  • money that moves, and messages that leave: emails, notifications, published documents;
  • releases to users where rollback is not instant, and changes to access rights;
  • new dependencies and new external services, which widen the supply chain;
  • any change that weakens or deletes a check (see Protect the attractor).

Where it sits. At the point of no return, not at the merge. Code merges when the checks pass. The gate fires when the irreversible act is about to happen: the migration runs, the payment leaves, the release starts. It guards the act, not the code.

What the person sees. Not a diff. A short packet the machines prepare, with four things:

  • what the action does, in the words of the promise;
  • which checks passed, including the held-out ones;
  • exactly what cannot be undone, and how much of it (rows, customers, amounts);
  • the rollback plan for everything else.

The person answers three questions. Is this the intended act? Is its reach acceptable? Is now the right time?

Who decides. The owner of the module’s attractor, or someone they name. For the largest actions, such as a schema change on customer data, a second person joins. Rotating the role spreads knowledge of the system.

How it stays thin. Every gated action is also a question for the people above the loop: could it be made reversible? Soft deletes instead of deletes, copies before migrations, feature flags and staged rollouts, dry runs, payments held for a review period. Each such change takes an action off the list. The measure is the share of changes that needed a person (Section 7). Take the simulation in Stop Reviewing Pull Requests: a team merging 40 changes a day. The thin gate and the daily audit together needed about 80 minutes of people time a day. Reviewing everything needed 16 hours.

What it is not. Not a code review, and not a signature on the code. The person at the gate is accountable for the act, as an operator is today when they press “run” on a migration.

6. People above the loop

Out of the loop is not out of the system. The human is still there, above the loop, guiding and structuring the whole. The level of the work changes, not its importance.

Above the loop, people:

  • Own the promise. Intent is the source; code is its output (Intent Is the Source). Understanding comes from the promise, not from diffs (Review the Intent, Not the Diff).
  • Own the checks. Not each test, which agents write, but what must always hold: properties, boundaries, budgets, security rules. They accept or refuse any change that would weaken a check.
  • Structure the system. Architecture and dependencies are the shape of the attractor. Once decided, they become checks.
  • Read the instruments. Escaped defects, mutation scores, loop times, drills, incidents. They change the system that produces code, not individual changes. That is what Fast AI argued: slow, careful work done once, so the fast path runs on its own.
  • Choose what to build and what to remove. Making is cheap; choosing is not (The Joy of the Question, Work Has Mass).

A person who reads a thousand lines a day guards a thousand lines. A person who turns “every endpoint has an authorisation test” into a check guards every line every agent will ever write there.

7. What it means for a team

The team owns the promise, the checks and the gates, not the text of the code. If it cannot regenerate a module from these, it does not really own it.

An example. A team adds “export invoices as CSV”.

  • Promise (a person, above the loop): customers can export their own invoices for a date range; amounts match the ledger.
  • Required checks (a person decides, an agent writes them):
    • for any date range, the exported total equals the ledger total;
    • no other customer’s invoice ever appears;
    • 10,000 invoices export in under two seconds;
    • every file parses with a standard CSV reader.
  • The loop (machines): the agent writes the code and extra tests. The required checks are read-only to it. Two held-out cases run outside its session.
  • Zone 3, found by a machine: a regeneration drill disagrees with the first version on customer names that contain a comma. The agent proposes a round-trip check (export, parse, compare). It adds a check, so it merges by rule.
  • The gate: none. Exporting reads data and can be undone. If the feature also emailed the file to customers, sending would be gated.
  • The people’s time: writing the promise and four checks, and later reading one drill report. Nobody read the code.
Table 3. What changes for a team.
Stop Start
Reading every generated diff as the default control Promise and required checks before the agent builds
Demanding reproducible generation (He and Thinking Machines Lab 2025) All checks inside the agent’s session
Style debates nobody depends on Machines that find zone 3: mutation tests, drills, usage-based contracts, daily audit
Asking “who wrote this line?” Asking “which check was missing?”
Counting lines reviewed Budgets for size, duplication and coupling (GitClear 2025)
A short, written list of gates

Roles. Each module has an owner of its attractor: the promise, the required checks, the gates. Security and platform engineers own the shared checks that every agent session runs. Nobody is accountable for reading every line.

Metrics.

  • Escaped defects, classified by zone (unchecked behaviour, or weak checks).
  • Mutation score: how firmly the checks hold.
  • Time from change to check result: how fast the loop pulls back.
  • Behaviour agreement between regenerations: how much of zone 3 is left.
  • Share of changes that needed a human gate: is it still a sliver?
  • Size, duplication and coupling trends: is the code gaining mass?

One test of the whole idea. Delete a module, and regenerate it from its promise and checks. Would you ship it unread? If not, your reasons are the checks you are missing. That list is the team’s real backlog.

Getting there. Building the attractor takes effort, and no team should drop review overnight. Here is a sequence that keeps the risk bounded:

  1. Pick one module with good tests. Measure its mutation score. Make its checks read-only to agents, and run them in the session.
  2. Write the gate list for it: the few actions that cannot be undone.
  3. Run regeneration drills, and add a check for every disagreement they find.
  4. Lower review step by step (every change, then a sample, then only the gate), as long as escaped defects and recovery time hold. If they do not, the missing checks show where.
  5. Repeat module by module. The share of changes that needs a person is the progress metric.

8. Security and the rest, in a nutshell

Security is not an exception. It is the most important part of zone 3, and it is held the same way.

Table 4. Security and other concerns: almost all held by machines, a few gated, the rest decided above the loop.
Concern How it is held Who acts
Injection, unsafe APIs, known weaknesses (Pearce et al. 2022) Static analysis and security linters in every session; security tests per entry point Machines
Secrets Secret scanning before anything leaves the session; no credentials in the agent’s reach Machines
Supply chain (Spracklen et al. 2025) Package and registry allowlists, lockfiles, provenance checks Machines; a person approves a new dependency
Access control, privacy Authorisation tests per endpoint; data-flow checks; policy as code Machines; people set the policy
The agent itself Sandboxed sessions; never private data, untrusted input and a way out at once (The Context Is the Wire) Machines enforce; people set the rule
Gaming the checks (Von Arx et al. 2025; Zhong et al. 2025) Read-only checks in the session; hidden held-out checks outside it; weakening or deleting a check needs a person Machines enforce; people approve
Irreversible actions Data deletion, payments, releases A person, at the gate
Performance, cost Latency, memory and cost budgets, checked like tests Machines; people set budgets
Maintainability Size, duplication and coupling budgets; architecture rules as tests Machines; people set the structure
Accessibility, licences, compliance Automated checks and scanners; rules as code Machines; people sample where automation is weak
Taste, fairness, product sense Judgement on the promise and the product People, above the loop

None of these depends on someone reading code. Human review missed them before AI too, and people using an assistant grew more confident, not more careful (Perry et al. 2023). A rule turned into a check protects every future change. A careful reading protects one.

9. Predictions and a test protocol

Each prediction says what would prove it wrong.

  • P1. Regeneration. Regenerated code differs widely. Behaviour agrees on the checks, and diverges elsewhere in proportion to what is unchecked. Refuted if code similarity predicts correct behaviour better than coverage and mutation score.
  • P2. Failures. Escaped defects in AI-written code come mostly from zone 3 or weak checks, not from “strange-looking code”. Refuted if reviewers’ sense of unusual code predicts them better.
  • P3. Automation beats reading. Effort on automated checks, mutation testing and drills prevents more escaped defects than the same effort on reading diffs. Refuted if reading prevents at least as many.
  • P4. Fast loops. The same checks, run in the agent’s session, reduce defects and rework compared with running them later in CI. Refuted if timing makes no difference.
  • P5. Teams. Teams that gate only the irreversible and work above the loop deliver at least as fast, with no more incidents. Refuted if, in a stepped-wedge trial as in Stop Reviewing Pull Requests, they have more incidents or slower recovery.

Protocol. For P1, P3 and P4, a regeneration study. Take modules with good tests. Regenerate each one twenty times under each condition: with review or without, checks in the session or in CI. Then compare three distances: between the programs, on the checks, and on random inputs by differential testing (McKeeman 1998). For P2 and P5, a stepped-wedge trial: teams adopt the rule one at a time, in random order. Escaped defects are classified by zone from incident reports, not from opinions about the code.

10. Objections, briefly

  • “Taking people out of the loop is reckless.” They leave the loop, not the system, and they keep the gate for the irreversible. The reckless default is the current one: a slow, partial check on every change, which does not reliably catch insecure code (Perry et al. 2023).
  • “This is randomness, not chaos.” The argument needs only two things: small input differences grow, and the code stays bounded. Generated code does both, at any temperature (Ouyang et al. 2023; He and Thinking Machines Lab 2025). The toy is deterministic on purpose: even perfect determinism would not restore control.
  • “The agents will game the checks.” They do when they can (Von Arx et al. 2025; Zhong et al. 2025). That is why the checks they see are read-only, the checks that decide are hidden from them, and any weakening of a check is a human decision. Reading diffs is a weaker defence: a test edited or special-cased to pass is easy to miss in a large change.
  • “Some behaviour cannot be checked.” True. For much behaviour, there is no cheap oracle that says what the right answer is (Barr et al. 2015). Partial oracles help: properties, metamorphic relations, differential tests against the previous version. What still cannot be checked stays above the loop, as a decision on the promise. Or it goes through a slower path with a person, until a check can be written. The thesis is about the default, not about every line.
  • “Regulated software requires review.” Some does. Airborne software certified under DO-178C, for example, requires reviews of the code (RTCA 2011). That is out of scope by design. Even there, automated checks can supply much of the evidence; the gate list is simply longer.
  • “Untested behaviour is the danger.” Yes, that is the thesis. It shows up when code runs, not when it is read. So machines that run the code in different ways find it, and a check holds it.
  • “Everything observable becomes depended on.” Hyrum’s law makes zone 3 grow over time (Winters et al. 2020). That cost exists for hand-written code too, and automation pays it best.
  • “People must still read code.” Sometimes: incidents, audits, domains without checks. Reading stays allowed. It just stops being the default control.
  • “Regenerations share blind spots.” They do (Knight and Leveson 1986). My claim is not that they give redundancy. It is that their differences are mostly harmless.
  • “Checks can be wrong.” An attractor is not a truth. That is why people belong above the loop, deciding what must hold, and not inside it.
  • “The evidence is mixed.” That is what I would expect from teams that adopted a chaotic generator but kept the old control (Peng et al. 2023; Becker et al. 2025; DORA 2024). P5 is the test.

11. Conclusion

The last year proved that machines can write working code. It has not yet changed where people stand while they do: at the output, reading.

Generated code is weather. The promised behaviour is climate. People do not need to forecast every gust. They decide the climate, build the instruments that keep it, and step in only where harm cannot be undone.

Automate the loop, gate a sliver, and move people above it. Let the code be chaotic: most of the chaos was never where the risk was.
