Work Has Mass

Scrum and SAFe are not wrong. In a heavier universe, they must get lighter.

essay
ai
agile
organisation
With AI, teams produce far more, and everything they produce must be carried. I treat that as mass that curves the space in which work moves, and argue that Scrum and SAFe should not grow to cope with it, but get lighter: keep what removes mass, cut what adds it.
Author

Stéphane Cuenat

Published

October 24, 2026

An outsider’s view. I know Scrum from years of practice; SAFe I know only from its public framework, and practitioners will know where real trains differ. The physics is a metaphor, used on purpose; the numbers come from a toy model described in full. Figures are my own, made with figs.py. This essay follows the Agentic Coding series, which looked at the code; here I look at the organisation around it.

TipThree laws of work mass
  1. Everything you make, you carry.
  2. AI lowers the cost of making, not of carrying.
  3. A process is mass too. In a heavier universe, frameworks must get lighter.

The wrong debate

Conversations about AI and agile frameworks fall into two camps. One says AI makes Scrum and SAFe obsolete: if an agent builds a feature in an afternoon, why plan in two-week sprints, or in ten-week increments with a hundred people in a room? The other says nothing changes: AI is just a faster tool inside a good process.

Both camps look at production: how fast work is made. But the frameworks were never only about production. They are about how a group of people carries a growing body of work over time without losing control of it. What should change them is not what AI makes, but what it leaves behind. And the answer, I will argue, is not more framework. It is less.

Every item has mass

Every item that enters the system stays for a while, and some stay for good: a story, a pull request, a feature, a test, a document, a dependency, an open decision, a backlog item someone will “get to later”. Each needs attention to keep: someone must understand it, maintain it, answer questions about it, or at least remember it is there.

I call that ongoing cost the item’s mass. Mass is not value. A small feature used by thousands can be light; a generated module nobody fully understands can be heavy.

In general relativity, mass curves spacetime: objects near a large mass follow curved paths, and time runs slower for them. That is a good picture of what accumulated work does to a team:

  • Paths bend. A new request is pulled toward the heavy parts of the system: the legacy module, the integration everyone depends on, the decision nobody has made.
  • Time slows near mass. The same change takes longer in a heavy system, because each step must account for more of what already exists.
  • There is a horizon. If mass keeps growing, all the team’s attention ends up carrying what exists. Requests still go in. Nothing comes out.

None of this is new. Lehman stated it as a law of software evolution: complexity grows unless work is spent to reduce it (Lehman 1980). Lean calls unfinished work inventory (Reinertsen 2009). Brooks showed that communication paths grow faster than headcount (Brooks 1975). What is new is the rate.

What AI changes: the cost of creating mass

AI makes producing an item much cheaper. It does not, or not yet, make carrying it much cheaper. Someone still has to decide whether a feature should exist, understand how it fits, own it when it breaks, and remember why it was built. As a balance:

\frac{dM}{dt} = \text{production} - \text{removal}, \qquad \text{capacity for new work} = H - k \cdot M,

where M is the mass of the system, H the team’s total attention and k the attention one unit of mass needs. Production has become cheap; removal was rarely planned at all. So M grows faster, and H - kM shrinks faster.

The smallest model I could build: a team has 100 hours of attention per sprint. The system starts with 250 items needing 0.1 hours each, so a quarter of the team already carries the past. A new item takes 6 hours to produce by hand or 1.5 with AI, plus 3 hours to absorb (review, decide, integrate). Whatever is left after carrying goes into new items, and every new item stays. These assumptions do most of the work; I return to them in the objections.

  • By hand, the team delivers 8.3 items in the first sprint and 6.3 in the last; carrying grows from 25% to 44% of its attention.
  • With AI, it delivers 16.7 items in the first sprint, and every dashboard looks wonderful. By the end of the year it delivers 9.5, with 58% of its attention on carrying. In the second year the gain is almost gone: 5.3 items per sprint against 4.7 by hand.
  • With AI and a mass budget, whenever carrying would exceed 35% of attention, the team removes items (deletes code, retires features, closes backlog items) before adding new ones. It delivers 13 items per sprint, in the first year and the second.

Over the first year the budget buys little: 356 items against 332, about 7% more. What differs is where each team stands at the end: one delivers 13 items a sprint with a third of its attention on the past, the other 9.5 and falling, with almost 60% on the past. AI does not change the shape of the problem, only its speed. A system that took a decade to become heavy can now do it in a year, and our frameworks were tuned, implicitly, for the decade.

Time dilation

Lead time depends on spare capacity. Queueing theory has known for sixty years that as load approaches capacity, waiting time does not grow steadily; it explodes (Kingman 1961; Little 1961). Mass eats spare capacity. Figure 3 adds one assumption to the model: requests arrive at a steady rate that needs 35% of the team.

This is consistent with what teams report: we produce more than ever, and everything takes longer. In 2024, DORA found more AI adoption associated with slightly lower delivery throughput (about 1.5%) and stability (about 7.2%) (DORA, Google Cloud 2024). In 2025, throughput turned positive but stability stayed negative, and the authors’ explanation is close to this essay’s: change volume outruns the controls downstream, and teams in tightly coupled systems see little or no benefit (DORA, Google Cloud 2025). Experienced developers were even found slower with early-2025 tools in their own large, mature repositories (Becker et al. 2025). None of these studies measured mass, but all point the same way: the gain from faster making is smallest where there is most to carry.

Measuring mass

Mass does not need to be precise to be seen. Two numbers are enough to start.

  • The carrying share. Tag every ticket, or every hour, as new or carry. Carry is everything spent on what already exists: support, incidents, upgrades, questions, reviews of changes that touch it. This is the x-axis of Figure 3.
  • The heaviest items. Once a quarter, attribute the carry hours to the items that caused them and list the top ten. Mass is very unequal; a few services, integrations or open decisions usually carry most of it. That list is where removal starts.

A framework is mass too

The third law is the one I find most important. Every event, role, artifact, metric and tool in a framework is an item in the system, and costs attention every sprint whether or not it helps. Frameworks grow by addition, and almost nothing is removed. When AI makes the work heavier, a heavier process is the last thing the system needs. So I would apply one test to every element of Scrum and SAFe:

The mass test. Does this element remove or prevent more mass than it adds?

My placements matter less than the pattern. The parts that say no pass. The parts that count fail. A Sprint Goal, a Definition of Done, a review, an iteration reserved for removal: each decides what not to carry, and costs little. Estimating every item, refining a long backlog, synchronising a large train: each manages a quantity of work, and quantity is exactly what AI has made cheap. Some elements sit in between and can be slimmed rather than cut. PI Planning is the clearest case, and SAFe is already moving it: its AI-Native guidance replaces the two-day event with a one-day PI Outcome Planning, and folds the System Demo and Inspect and Adapt into a two-hour event each iteration (Scaled Agile, Inc. 2026). Applied honestly, the test does not reject either framework. It shrinks both to their core.

Scrum: lighter, not looser

The Scrum Guide is already short: empiricism and lean thinking, a team of ten or fewer, a time-box of one month or less, five events and three commitments, the Product Goal, the Sprint Goal and the Definition of Done (Schwaber and Sutherland 2020). It says nothing about story points or velocity; those are common add-ons, and most of what makes Scrum heavy in practice lives in the add-ons.

Table 1. Scrum, read again with mass in mind. The middle column is my reading of the Scrum Guide and of common practice; the right column is my proposal.
Element Calibrated for In a heavier universe
Sprint A rhythm to produce a usable increment A rhythm to absorb one: decide, integrate, understand.
Sprint Goal Focus for the sprint Keep. The cheapest way to say no.
Definition of Done The quality an increment must meet Keep, and add: what it costs to carry, what was removed to make room.
Product Backlog An ordered list, refined as needed Short, with items that expire. AI can fill it without limit.
Sprint Review Inspect the increment Inspect the increment and the mass ledger (Figure 6).
Daily Scrum Daily inspection toward the goal Keep it short, or async for a small team.
Story points, velocity (add-ons, not in the Guide) How much the team can make Drop. With AI they measure mass added. Use lead time and the carrying share.
Team size Ten or fewer, to keep communication manageable The limit is the mass each person carries. Five people with AI can carry the mass of twenty.

The backlog needs evaporation. When writing a backlog item took effort, the effort was a filter. Now an AI can turn every meeting into ten well-written stories, and every story is a promise with mass. Untouched for three sprints, an item expires unless someone renews it. Black holes slowly evaporate. Backlogs should too.

What remains is Scrum at its core: a goal, a standard, a short list, a rhythm of inspection, and a ledger of what the team carries. Less framework than most teams run today, not more.

SAFe: smaller trains, fewer layers

SAFe’s central structure is the Agile Release Train (ART), a long-lived team of agile teams, generally 50 to 125 people (Scaled Agile, Inc. 2023), that plans together every 8 to 12 weeks (Scaled Agile, Inc., n.d.-b). Work is ranked with Weighted Shortest Job First (WSJF): cost of delay divided by job size, an idea from product development flow (Reinertsen 2009).

SAFe already knows that existing work has weight. At each interval boundary, trains agree on a capacity allocation: the share of capacity for new features against enablers, technical debt and maintenance (Scaled Agile, Inc., n.d.-a). That is close to a mass budget, and trains that use it well are ahead. The difference is what is limited. A capacity allocation caps the effort spent on old work; a mass budget caps the old work itself, and forces removal when it grows.

WSJF stops discriminating

With AI, job sizes shrink, but unevenly: small features hit a floor, complex ones shrink less. The denominator loses its spread, and the ranking collapses toward cost of delay alone. And job size never measured what a feature costs to carry, which is now the larger part.

The fix is small: add the carrying cost to the denominator,

\text{WSJF}_{\text{mass}} = \frac{\text{cost of delay}}{\text{job size} + \text{carrying cost}},

where carrying cost is a relative estimate of the attention the feature will need in its first year. A chat assistant is quick to build and slow to carry, so it falls; a faster search adds almost nothing to carry, so it rises. And it is enough to score the top of the backlog: ranking features that will never be built is itself mass.

Smaller trains, fewer layers

50 to 125 people is a coordination limit, learned from teams who produced at human speed. But a train does not coordinate people; it coordinates what they produce and must keep consistent: interfaces, dependencies, shared data, shared decisions. If each person adds three times more mass, a train reaches that limit at about a third of the size.

The tempting answer is more structure. The mass test points the other way. Split trains by mass, long before 125 people, so that each carries less. Smaller trains need fewer sync meetings and fewer coordinating roles, and may need less of the structure above them. Planning shrinks with them: each team brings a mass budget next to its capacity, the board shows only the dependencies that cross teams, and the Innovation and Planning iteration becomes the home of removal. SAFe with AI should look less like a bigger SAFe and more like a few small, light trains, each with a clear budget of what it is allowed to carry.

What I would actually do

  1. Measure the carrying share and list the ten heaviest items.
  2. Set a mass budget, and treat crossing it like a red build: stop adding, start removing.
  3. Make removal a deliverable, shown at the Sprint Review and System Demo next to what was added.
  4. Let items expire: backlog items, open questions, generated documents.
  5. Price mass into priority: WSJF with carrying cost, for the top of the backlog only.
  6. Size teams and trains by mass, not headcount.
  7. Put the framework through the mass test, these seven proposals included.

That last point matters, because these are new practices and an essay against framework mass should not quietly add some. Each one replaces something heavier. The carrying share and lead time replace story points and velocity. Expiry replaces the refinement of a long backlog. The ledger replaces a status report, and is one chart in a review that already exists. The mass budget is a number in a planning event that already exists, not a new event. If one of them does not remove more than it adds in your team, drop it.

Objections

“This is technical debt, or Lean inventory, with a new name.” Partly. But a well-built feature that people use is neither debt nor inventory, and it must still be carried. Lean’s inventory is mostly unfinished work; the mass that grows fastest with AI is finished work. Lean also says to manage a system at its constraint (Goldratt and Cox 1984), and the constraint has moved from making to carrying.

“AI will carry the mass too.” In part, yes: agents already read code and summarise documents, which lowers k. If k falls as fast as production costs, this essay is wrong. I doubt it will, because the mass that matters most needs a person: accountability for a feature, the decision to keep or kill it, the customer who depends on it.

“Nobody gets promoted for deleting things.” True, and the strongest reason a mass budget may fail. Organisations reward what is added. That is why removal must be shown and counted at the same events as delivery, and why the ledger has two columns. What is not seen is not rewarded.

“The model is too simple.” It is: one team, constant parameters, items that never get lighter, and an AI that lowers the cost of making but not of carrying. The model shows the consequence of those assumptions, not their truth. If a better one shows mass levelling off on its own, I would like to see it.

“The physics is decoration.” Perhaps. But metaphors choose what we look at. Velocity made teams look at speed. Mass would make them look at what speed leaves behind.

Conclusion

Scrum and SAFe are not wrong. Empiricism, short feedback loops, small teams, shared cadence and economic prioritisation are as useful with AI as without it. But both were calibrated in a universe where producing work was the hard part, and where mass grew slowly enough to be someone else’s problem later.

AI has changed the rate at which we create mass, not yet the rate at which we carry it. The reflex will be to add: more ceremonies to control more output, more roles to coordinate more teams, more layers to plan more work. That adds mass to a system already falling into its own well. The better answer is the opposite: measure the mass, budget it, price it, remove it as deliberately as we add, and then put the frameworks themselves on the scale.

Everything you make, you carry. In a heavier universe, start by carrying less framework.
