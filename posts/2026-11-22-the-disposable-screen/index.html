The Disposable Screen

Today’s interfaces exist so that people can drive the backend by hand. An AI agent drives it directly. For most tasks the screen is no longer needed, and when it is, the agent draws it for the moment.

An AI agent that uses the backend directly needs almost no screen. When one is needed, it is drawn for that moment, not designed in advance.
Author

Stéphane Cuenat

Published

November 22, 2026

Essay, November 2026. A short and deliberately provocative essay about the screens we build for software, and why they will matter much less than they do today. It is a bet, not a forecast with numbers. Where I state a fact, I give the source. The three figures are drawings of my own, and the code that draws them is linked under each one.

In short. We spend months designing screens for our software, and each person then uses a small part of them. That effort is about to lose most of its reason. A screen exists so that a person can drive the backend by hand: the functions that do the work, and the data they use. An AI agent drives those functions directly. For most tasks, no screen is needed at all. When a person must see something or decide, the agent draws a screen for that moment. It shows the facts and the one button the decision needs, and then it is thrown away. The generic screen, defined in advance and the same for everyone, stops being the centre of software. Habit, trust and the need to explore will keep some fixed screens alive, but they will be the exception. What matters now is the backend, described well, and the rules about what a person must always see.

1. The screen nobody needed

Today, building software is, for a large part, building screens. Teams run workshops, draw mock-ups, test with users and argue about where a button goes. The result is coded page by page and maintained for years.

Then watch what happens. One person opens the screen to change an address. Another approves one invoice. A third exports a report once a quarter. Each one touches a few fields and one button. The rest is there for someone else.

So the screen is a compromise: one drawing, defined in advance, for everyone and every task. For the average user it may be the best interface. For any one person on any one day, most of it is useless.

2. The screen was only the way in

A screen is not the software. It is a way in. Behind every button sits a function in the backend: find this invoice, check that supplier, send this payment. The screen exists because, until now, a person had to drive those functions by hand, one click at a time.

That is ending. In November 2024, Anthropic published the Model Context Protocol, an open standard that lets an AI agent find and call the functions of other systems (Anthropic 2024b). A service describes its functions as data: what each one does, what it needs, what it returns. I wrote about this in The Interface Is Data. The agent reads the description and calls the functions itself. No menu, no page, no form.

Jakob Nielsen, who has studied usability for decades, saw the shift coming in 2023. He called AI the first new way of using computers in sixty years: you say what you want, and the machine works out how (Nielsen 2023). Satya Nadella, the head of Microsoft, went further at the end of 2024. He described most business applications as databases with business rules on top, and said those rules are moving to agents (Windows Central 2024). If so, what remains of an application is its backend. The pages in front of it were built for people driving by hand.

Even the detour proves the point. In 2024, Anthropic also taught its model to use a computer like a person: look at the screen, move the cursor, click (Anthropic 2024a). That helps when a system has no other way in. But it is a bridge, not a destination. Once the backend is open, the agent has no reason to look at screens made for humans.

3. A screen for the moment

A person still needs to see a result, choose between options or confirm a payment. For that, the agent draws the screen itself. In 2025, researchers at Google showed that a language model can build a custom, interactive interface for almost any request (Google Research 2025). People preferred these screens to plain text answers more than eight times out of ten. They matched screens made by human experts in about half of the cases, though each one took a minute or two to draw (Leviathan et al. 2026). The same year, OpenAI let services show small interactive apps inside its chat assistant (OpenAI 2025). And the Model Context Protocol gained a standard way for a tool to send an interface with its answer (Model Context Protocol 2025).

So here is my claim, without hedging. For most tasks, there will be no screen. When one is needed, it is drawn for that moment and thrown away. The screen becomes disposable. And the generic screen we define in advance stops being something we need to build.

Much goes with it:

  • Navigation. Menus, tabs and sidebars exist to find your corner of a big screen. When the agent finds the function, there is no corner to find.
  • The app as a place. You will not go to the software. It will come to you, as a small screen inside what you are already doing, and leave when you are done.
  • The general form. Forty fields, of which you fill four. The agent knows the other thirty-six.
  • Automation that clicks. Robots that copy values from one window to another, because two systems cannot talk, break as soon as a button moves. An agent that calls the backend has nothing to break.
  • One page per feature. The team builds the function, describes it well, and stops there.

In Intent Is the Source I argued that for programmers, intent becomes the source and code becomes an output. This is the same move for everyone else. Intent is the input, and the screen is an output, when there is one at all.

4. Four objections that do not save the screen

Habit. People want to know where things are. In 2004, Leah Findlater and Joanna McGrenere found that a menu that never changed was faster than one that rearranged itself. Most people preferred a menu they arranged themselves (Findlater and McGrenere 2004). True, for what you do every day. There, the agent draws the screen once and keeps it. But most of our habits exist to find our way through navigation, and navigation is what disappears. The study’s real lesson is that people want a screen shaped to them and under their control. A screen drawn for the moment is exactly that.

Trust. A bank or a hospital needs the same flow every time, tested and certified. This is the strongest objection, and regulated flows will keep fixed screens the longest. But you test the rules, not the pictures. Before any payment, show the amount, the recipient and the date, and ask for a clear confirmation. A program, not the agent, checks that rule on every screen before it is shown, as I proposed for tools in A Grammar for Tools. And the exact screen a person saw can be recorded. That is better evidence than “it was version 4.2 of the form”.

Typing. We left the command line for good reasons, and many people find it hard to put what they want into words (Nielsen 2023). In 1997, Ben Shneiderman defended direct manipulation against Pattie Maes, who defended agents (Shneiderman and Maes 1997). In 2025, Andrej Karpathy said that graphical interfaces still matter because they let a person check an AI’s work quickly (Karpathy 2025). Agreed, and this is not chat. The request may be a sentence or a click. What comes back is a real screen, made to check this one result. It is direct manipulation, drawn by an agent, so both sides of 1997 win.

Looking. A map, a canvas or a code editor is for exploring, not for finishing a task. These instruments stay; a violin is not rebuilt for each song. But even a dashboard, as I argued in Show What Changed, Not the Data, should show only what changed. It is different every week: a disposable screen.

So the objections do not save today’s screen. They mark out a small territory where fixed screens remain: daily routines, regulated decisions and instruments. Everywhere else, the agent works through the backend.

5. Where the work goes

The backend comes first. Its functions are now the product. They must be described well enough for an agent to use them safely: what each one does, what it needs, what it changes, and whether it is safe to retry.

Then the parts and the rules. The parts are tested buttons, fields and charts, usable by everyone. The rules say what must always be shown: the amount before a payment, the dose before a prescription. Google’s A2UI project, opened in December 2025, works this way (Google for Developers 2025). The agent sends a description of the interface as data, and the app draws it only from a catalogue of parts it trusts.

Designers do not disappear. Kate Moran and Sarah Gibbons, of the Nielsen Norman Group, call this outcome-oriented design (Moran and Gibbons 2024). Designers set the goals and the limits, and the system composes the screen. Almost nobody sets type by hand anymore, but someone still designs every typeface we read.

And there is a gift in it. A screen drawn for one person can be drawn for that person’s eyes and language from the start, with large text, fewer choices and plain words. That will only happen if the rules ask for it.

6. The test

Forecasts point both ways. In 2024, the research firm Gartner predicted that by 2028 a third of enterprise software would include AI agents. A year later, it predicted that more than forty percent of agentic AI projects would be cancelled by the end of 2027 (Gartner 2025). So here is my own test. If, five years from now, most work still starts by opening an app, finding a menu and filling in a fixed form, I was wrong. If not, the change will have happened quietly. In 1991, the computer scientist Mark Weiser wrote: “The most profound technologies are those that disappear” (Weiser 1991). The screen is next.

For anyone about to design a new screen, one question. Could an agent do this task through the backend, and show the person only what they must see, when they must see it? If yes, don’t build the screen. Build the backend, the parts and the rules.

7. A map on a napkin

Ask for directions in a café, and someone draws you a map on a napkin: three streets, a church, an arrow. It is not pretty and not to scale. It shows only your way, and it is exactly what you need. When you arrive, you throw it away.

For decades we have printed better and better city maps, the same for everyone, and asked each person to find their way on them. The next interface is the napkin. Most of the time, someone simply takes you there. When you need to see the way, it is drawn for you, right now, and thrown away when you arrive.
