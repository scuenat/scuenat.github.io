Long First, Short Later

Two proofs of the Riemann hypothesis in one day, one with a machine and one by hand. Why I think this is good news, and why we will still be needed to make them short.

Two claimed proofs of the Riemann hypothesis in one day, one made with Claude, one by hand. Both are long. Why I think the short proof will still need us.
Author

Stéphane Cuenat

Published

November 21, 2026

Essay, November 2026. A short, personal essay, written the morning after two claimed proofs of the Riemann hypothesis appeared on the same day. Neither has been confirmed, and this essay does not judge either one. I can’t. It is about what the day may change in how we do mathematics. The facts about the two claims come from press reports of 20 November 2026, and I say whose. The second figure is computed; the others are drawings of my own. The code that draws them is linked under each one.

In short. On the same day, two proofs of the most famous open problem in mathematics were announced. One was made in 41 days with an AI, Claude, and so far only a computer has checked it. The other was written by hand over twelve years, and it runs to 940 pages. I find this amazing, and I think it will change how mathematics is done, without removing mathematics, or us. Both proofs are long, and that is normal: the first proof of a hard theorem usually is, and the short one comes later. So here is my bet. Machines will give us more long proofs, faster than ever. Our part will be to find the short ones inside them. I can’t prove that a short proof always exists, and some problems may need mathematics that does not exist yet. But I believe we live in a universe that can be described simply, and inventing that new mathematics is, I think, the same work as finding the short proof.

1. Two proofs before breakfast

This morning, a little before eight, I was reading LinkedIn on my phone. A professor of mathematics had posted a summary of the day before. Its title was Two Riemann Proofs, One Day. I read it twice.

Here is what happened, as reported. Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, announced that he and Claude had proved the Riemann hypothesis, and a wider version of it too. It took 41 days. There is no paper. There is a short note, and an archive of 175,187 files written in Lean. Lean is a programming language in which a computer checks every step of a proof. In a footnote, he says that a full version written out in prose ran past 250,000 pages, and that he chose not to post it. Andrew Sutherland, a mathematician at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, built the archive on his own computers. It compiles: the machine accepts every step. The next check, that the final statement really says what the Riemann hypothesis says, was still under way (Scientific American 2026; OfficeChai 2026).

A few hours later, Ravi Vakil, the president of the American Mathematical Society, posted a second claim. It is a 940-page manuscript called The Music of the Primes, by a young mathematician, Alessandro Masullo. Twelve years of work, all human, no AI. Brian Conrad at Stanford has been reading it closely for months. The plan had been to polish it quietly for a year. The first announcement changed that, so the draft went out as it was (TechCrunch 2026; Nacionale 2026). Conrad was careful. To judge the method, he said, the paper must reach a more final version. It is “undergoing major revision” (Scientific American 2026). An early audit posted online already claims serious problems in it; that is not confirmed either (Refine 2026).

So, two proofs, and neither is confirmed. The professor ended the post with a question: what is a proof for? I wrote a short comment under it. This essay is that comment, given a bit more room.

2. What both proofs are about

The Riemann hypothesis is 167 years old. Bernhard Riemann stated it in 1859, in a paper of about ten pages (Riemann 1859). It is about the prime numbers: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, the numbers that can only be divided by one and by themselves.

If you count the primes as you go up, the count climbs like a staircase. Each prime is a step. The steps look irregular. But from far away, the staircase follows a smooth curve, one that Gauss guessed as a teenager. Riemann found that the gap between the staircase and the curve is controlled by a special function, the zeta function. More exactly, by the places where it is zero. Each zero adds a small wave, like a note in a chord. Together, the notes play the primes. That is why Masullo called his manuscript The Music of the Primes.

The hypothesis says that all these notes sit on one straight line. Computers have checked trillions of them, and every one is on the line (Platt and Trudgian 2021). But checking is not proving. Nobody has shown that it must be so for all of them. If it is true, many things we believe about the primes become certain. Hundreds of results in mathematics begin with the words “assuming the Riemann hypothesis”.

So this is not just another puzzle. It is the problem many mathematicians would pick if they could solve only one.

3. Both are long, and that is normal

What struck me first was not the machine. It was the length, by hand as much as by machine. So length is not only a machine habit. People do it too, and there is a reason. The first proof of a hard theorem is almost always long. The first person through a forest does not walk in a straight line. They go around, they backtrack, they cut through brambles. The straight path is found later, by people who already know where the other side is.

The prime number theorem is my favourite example, because it is about the same staircase. It says that the staircase follows Gauss’s curve. Jacques Hadamard and Charles de la Vallée Poussin proved it in 1896, each on their own, with heavy tools. Hadamard’s paper was 22 pages (Hadamard 1896). In 1980, Donald Newman found a proof that fits in four pages of a journal for students and teachers (Newman 1980). Don Zagier later showed it could be told even more briefly (Zagier 1997). Eighty-four years, from a forest to a path.

There are other stories like it. In 1976, Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken proved the four colour theorem with a computer checking more than a thousand cases, one by one (Appel and Haken 1977). Many mathematicians were uneasy: they could not check it by hand. Almost thirty years later, Georges Gonthier checked the whole proof in a proof assistant, a program that verifies every step (Gonthier 2008). The proof of the Kepler conjecture, about the best way to stack oranges, went the same way. Referees could not fully check it, so Thomas Hales and his colleagues checked it by machine (Hales et al. 2017).

So the long proof is not new. What is new is the speed. A machine can now walk through the forest in 41 days instead of twelve years.

4. “Then we are finished”

Now, here is the strongest objection, and I want to give it its full weight.

It goes like this. If a machine can prove the Riemann hypothesis in 41 days, what is left for mathematicians? Twelve years of a life against six weeks of a computer. The machine will not stop at one problem. It will take the next one, and the next. Soon, the human proof will be a hobby, like knitting your own socks. Beautiful, but not needed.

I feel the force of this. And I understand why Masullo wrote, in his introduction, to the mathematician “who feels discouraged by the pace and tone of the current climate”. His answer: “We are still here. We are very much alive. We are not going anywhere” (TechCrunch 2026).

I agree with him, but for a reason that has less to do with defiance. It has to do with what a proof is for.

A proof does two jobs. The first is to convince us that a statement is true. A machine can now do that job, and do it better than we can: it does not get tired at page 600. The second job is to make us understand why it is true. That second job is the one that moves mathematics forward. The understanding is what you carry to the next problem.

William Thurston, one of the great geometers of the last century, said it more clearly than I can. He wrote: “We are not trying to meet some abstract production quota of definitions, theorems and proofs. The measure of our success is whether what we do enables people to understand and think more clearly and effectively about mathematics” (Thurston 1994).

A proof of 175,187 files that nobody can read does the first job. It does not yet do the second. Alpöge himself, as reported, has not yet mastered all the mathematics around it (OfficeChai 2026). That is not a criticism. It is the honest state of a first proof. The forest has been crossed. Nobody has drawn the map yet.

So I don’t think we are finished. I think our work changes: less time hacking through the brambles, more time looking at the path the machine took.

5. The two routes need each other

The two proofs that appeared on the same day are not rivals. Each one needs exactly what the other one has.

Masullo’s 940 pages need checking. Conrad has spent months on them, and still the paper is under major revision. Machines could help here: turn the 940 pages into Lean, step by step, and let the computer find the gaps. That is exactly what happened with the four colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture, only it took years. It could now take weeks.

Alpöge’s 175,187 files need the opposite. They need people to read them, not line by line, but the way a hiker reads a map. Where does the path climb? Where does it turn? Which loop was a detour? Machines can help with that too. But deciding which idea is the real one, the one worth teaching, is a judgement about what matters. I think that judgement stays with us for a long time.

So the change is not bad. It is a new division of work. The machine is a tireless explorer. We become the cartographers.

6. My bet: it must be simpler

Now I have to be honest. Everything above assumes that a short proof of the Riemann hypothesis exists. I don’t know that. Nobody does.

Logic even warns us. In 1936, Kurt Gödel noted that some true statements have only very long proofs in a given system, far longer than anyone could write. Step into a stronger system, a wider point of view, and the same statements can have short proofs (Gödel 1936). So there is no guarantee that every theorem has a short proof. And the Riemann hypothesis might be one of the long ones.

But notice when the proof became short: when the point of view changed, not when someone worked harder in the same place. I find that hopeful.

And here is where I am provocative, I know. I don’t have the answer. I am not a mathematician. But I believe we live in a universe that can be described simply. Not because simplicity is guaranteed, but because it has worked so often that it would be strange if it stopped now. A few equations describe light. A few more describe gravity. The physicist Eugene Wigner called this “the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences” (Wigner 1960). G. H. Hardy wrote that “there is no permanent place in the world for ugly mathematics” (Hardy 1940). Paul Erdős liked to joke that God keeps a book with the most elegant proof of every theorem. Two mathematicians, Martin Aigner and Günter Ziegler, even tried to write some of its pages down (Aigner and Ziegler 2018).

Something as orderly as the prime staircase, I believe, must have a reason that can be said in a few pages. Maybe not today. Maybe not by me. The long proofs are the first step toward it, not the last.

I wrote about this feeling before, in a different setting. One evening I read a physics paper I could not follow. A voice in my head said: it must be simpler than this (It Must Be Simpler Than This). A 250,000-page proof gives me exactly the same feeling. Not that it is wrong. That it is not finished.

7. Where the data runs out

There is a second challenge, and this one I raise against myself. Machines may be very good at finding solutions where there is already a lot of material around the problem. It may be different for a conjecture whose proof needs mathematics that does not exist yet.

The Riemann hypothesis is not virgin ground. There is a huge literature around it, trillions of zeros computed, decades of partial results, and a large library of mathematics already written in Lean. A machine that works on it works inside a map that people drew. It has examples to learn from, tools to reuse, and a checker that tells it when it is wrong.

Some conjectures need something that is on no map yet. History has many of these moments. To understand why some equations have no formula for their solutions, Évariste Galois had to invent a new idea, what we now call a group. To prove Fermat’s Last Theorem, Andrew Wiles had to prove a large part of a bridge between two distant parts of mathematics. Others had only guessed that the bridge was there. In those cases, the hard part was not to search longer. It was to see the problem in a language that did not exist yet.

I don’t know whether machines can do that. Maybe they already do it, somewhere inside those 175,187 files, and nobody has seen it yet. If so, the people who distil the proof will be the first to find out. But it may also be that machines are at their best where there is a lot to learn from, and weaker where there is nothing like it. I said something close to this in April, and I was partly wrong (Partly Wrong, Mostly Right). So I hold it loosely.

Here is what strikes me, though. Very often, a new piece of mathematics is exactly what Gödel’s result hinted at: a new point of view that makes an old difficulty disappear. If so, the work I am betting on is finding the short proof. The work machines may find hardest is inventing new mathematics. And those may be one and the same work. That is not a reason to relax. It is a reason to keep doing mathematics ourselves.

8. What a proof is for

So here is where I land, the morning after.

A machine may have crossed a forest that people have walked around for 167 years. On the same day, a person crossed it too, by hand, after twelve years. Whatever the verdicts, I find both amazing, and I think mathematics will be done differently tomorrow. But it changes our job; it does not remove it. The first proof tells us that something is true. The short proof tells us why. Finding the short one has always been human work, and I think the long machine proofs will give us more of that work, not less.

The professor asked what a proof is for. My answer is the one I wrote under the post, a little before eight this morning. A proof is for turning a truth into an idea that one mind can hold.

The machine wrote the first draft. Now comes the part where we make it short.
