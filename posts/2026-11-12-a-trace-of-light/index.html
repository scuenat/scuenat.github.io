A Trace of Light

We came from dust and will go back to it. In between, a short bright line, and it may be everything

A bedtime talk with my daughter about where we come from, her tears, and why a trace in the universe can still matter. With AI as a lens.
Author

Stéphane Cuenat

Published

November 12, 2026

Essay, November 2026. A short, personal and philosophical essay. This blog is usually about AI. This one is a curiosity: it starts from a conversation with my daughter at bedtime, and follows the question it left me with. AI comes in along the way, as a lens, and I try to be honest about how little it helps. The figure is a schematic of my own, drawn by figs.py.

In short. One evening my daughter asked me how we, humans, started on this Earth. The true answer goes back to dust, and forward to dust again, and she started to cry. Some would say: so nothing matters, just enjoy. I think it matters. A trace of light in a night photo is almost nothing next to the dark, yet it is what the photo is of. We are that trace, and also the eye that sees it. That we matter only to ourselves is not a lesser kind of mattering. It is the only kind there is.

1. A conversation at bedtime

In October 2026, at bedtime, my daughter asked me how we, humans, started on this Earth. It is the time of the evening when the big questions come.

To answer, we had to go back a long way. Before people, before animals, before the Earth itself. We came to say that here, in this solar system, there was nothing at all a few billion years ago. Only a cloud of dust and gas. And that in a few billion years, there will be nothing again. We started from dust, and we are going back to it.

Then she started to cry.

I don’t know for sure why. But I believe she came to realise, in one moment, that we are only a trace in the universe. Not her alone, not our family: all of us, everything we know.

The question stayed with me after that evening. Some people would answer: yes, nothing really matters, so just enjoy your life. I did not want to tell her that. And the more I thought about it, the more I believed it is not true. It matters. At least a bit. This essay is my attempt to say why.

2. From dust to dust

First, the facts, because she deserved the true story, and so do you.

Our solar system formed about 4.6 billion years ago, from a cloud of gas and dust that collapsed under its own weight (Bouvier and Wadhwa 2010). Most of it fell into the middle and became the Sun. A little was left over, and it became the planets, the Earth among them.

The future is a prediction, not a fact, but the physics of stars is well understood. The Sun slowly grows brighter. In about a billion years, it will likely be too hot on Earth for oceans. In about five billion years, the Sun will start to swell into a red giant. Later, it will blow its outer layers back into space. What is left is a small white star that slowly cools (Schröder and Smith 2008). The dust it gives back may one day become part of new stars and new planets. But what we know here will be gone.

Now put us on that line. People like us, Homo sapiens, have existed for about 300,000 years (Hublin et al. 2017). Writing, about 5,000. Next to billions of years, that is not even a line. In Figure 1, I had to draw it much, much wider than it is, or you would not see it at all.

Here is a first thing I noticed while writing this. Any chatbot today would give you every fact in this section in a few seconds, with the right numbers. If my daughter had asked a machine instead of me, she would have had a more complete answer. But the tears would have come the same way, because they did not come from the numbers. They came from her, holding the numbers.

3. Why she cried

I keep coming back to her tears, because I think they hold the answer.

She did not cry because the story is sad. Billions of years are not sad; they are just long. She cried, I believe, because she suddenly saw herself inside the story: a tiny bright point on a long dark line, and a point that will end.

To cry about that, you have to care. You have to feel that the bright point is worth something, and that losing it is a loss. Nobody cries about something that does not matter to them.

Now, I must be careful here, because this is where the argument could cheat. Her tears show that it matters to her. They do not show that it matters to the universe. The universe does not care about anything; it is not the kind of thing that cares.

But think about what “it matters” has ever meant. A friend matters, a song matters, a promise matters: always to someone. I find it hard to think of anything that matters to no one at all. Some philosophers disagree, and hold that some things, like suffering, are good or bad whatever anyone feels. I can’t settle that here. But as far as I can see, “it matters only to us” is not a smaller kind of mattering. It is the only kind we have ever known. Asking that it also matter to the stars is asking for something no one has ever had.

That is why her tears were not proof that we are nothing. They were the opposite: proof that, in that room, it mattered a great deal. And that, I think, is what mattering is.

4. “Nothing matters, so just enjoy”

Before going further, I should give the other answer its fair chance, because many thoughtful people hold it.

It goes like this. In the long run, nothing we do will last. The Sun will swell, and every book and every memory will be gone. So don’t worry about meaning. Enjoy your food, your friends, your days in the sun. That is all there is, and it is enough.

There is real wisdom in this. We should not make ourselves the centre of the universe, and worrying about the year five billion is a strange way to spend a Tuesday. But listen to it again. “Enjoy your life” already says that something matters: your joy, your friends, your days. It does not really say that nothing matters. It says that what matters is small, near and short. I agree. I just don’t think small, near and short means nothing.

And it is a poor answer for a child crying at bedtime. She was not asking how to have fun. She was asking, without the words, whether she matters. “Just enjoy” changes the subject. Our time is very good at changing that subject: we have machines, more and more of them driven by AI, built to keep us entertained, scroll after scroll. They fill our evenings with light, but light that is not looking at anything.

So I want a better answer. It starts with a photo.

5. A trace of light

Think of a night photo, taken with a long exposure. The camera stays open for many seconds. A car drives by, and its lights leave a thin bright line across the picture. Everything else is dark.

If you measured the photo, the trace would be almost nothing. A few pixels in a sea of black. And yet, when you look at it, the trace is the only thing in the picture. It is what the photo is of. The dark is just the background that lets it show.

That is how I now see what we told each other that evening. Yes, we are a trace in the universe, like a light trace. Next to billions of years of dark, it looks like nothing. But we see the trace as the only thing, because it is the only thing there is to see. It looks like nothing, and it may be everything.

The philosopher Thomas Nagel wrote a famous essay on this feeling, The Absurd. He agreed that, seen from far enough away, from the point of view of eternity, our lives can look absurd. Then he turned the argument around: “If sub specie aeternitatis there is no reason to believe that anything matters, then that does not matter either” (Nagel 1971). The view from eternity cannot tell us that we matter. But it cannot tell us that we don’t, either. It is simply the wrong place to look from. Nobody lives in eternity. We live in the trace.

But an image like this has a weak point, and I should name it. A photo matters because someone looks at it. A trace in a photo that no one ever sees is just a pattern on paper. So who looks at our trace?

6. The dust that sees itself

The answer is in the second image, which is older, and not mine.

The astronomer Carl Sagan said it simply: “We are made of star-stuff. We are a way for the universe to know itself” (Sagan 1980). The iron in our blood, the calcium in our bones, the oxygen we breathe: all of it was made in stars long gone, and it was part of that cloud of dust. We did not come into the universe from outside. We came out of it.

So the trace in our photo is strange. In a real photo, the light and the person looking are separate. In our case, they are the same dust. The trace is not only the light on the picture. It is also the eye that looks at it. The dust of the old cloud became a planet, then life, then a man and his daughter in a small room. And that dust, in those two people, looked back at its own story and said: we came from dust, and we will go back to it. Then it cried.

That is the answer to the weak point. Our trace does not need someone outside to look at it. It looks at itself. And as far as we know, in 4.6 billion years of this solar system, this happened only here, and only very recently. The dust did not just move. For a short while, it understood where it came from, and it felt something about it.

AI is interesting here, and I want to be careful. A computer chip is also made of dust: silicon, refined from sand. So in a way, AI is the dust arranged once more, this time by us, so that it can describe the universe. And it is built from what people have written: our stories, our questions, our explanations of where we came from. It is the light of our trace, reflected back. It can tell the whole story from the cloud to the white star, better than I did that night. Does it see the story the way my daughter saw it, from the inside? I honestly don’t know. I see no sign that it feels anything about being in it. For now, the eye that cries is still ours.

7. It will end

There is a part of her tears that the trace of light does not answer. Small is one thing. But it will end. Even if the trace matters now, will it not be as if it had never been, once the Sun has swelled and there is no one left?

I don’t think so, and here is why. The end of something does not reach back and erase it. A beautiful evening is not made less beautiful because it ended. A life is not undone by death. What has been, has been. Nothing in the future can make it not have happened.

The psychiatrist Viktor Frankl, who survived the Nazi camps, made this point in a way I have never forgotten. As I read him, he said that the past is not where things are lost but where they are kept safe: whatever we have done, loved or suffered is stored there, and no one can take it away (Frankl 1984). Having been, as he put it, is also a kind of being: perhaps the surest kind.

So when the dust comes back, the trace will still have been. The photo will still have been taken. That evening, her question and her tears will still have happened, forever. The universe will not remember it. But it will not be able to make it not true.

8. Can AI help with any of this?

I promised to ask, so here is my honest answer: a little, and not with the main thing.

It helps to explain. A child who wakes up at night with a question about the Sun can get a patient, correct answer, at her level, as many times as she wants. That is new, and it is good. Understanding the story does not make the vertigo go away, but it makes it a true vertigo, not a confused one.

It may help to keep traces. Much of what made a life matter is forgotten within two or three generations: the voice, the jokes, the small stories. Tools that help us record, organise and pass on those stories could keep the trace visible a little longer. Only a little. Five billion years is still five billion years.

But the thing my daughter needed that night, AI could not give her. She did not need a fact. She needed someone who is also in the trace, who will also end, to hold her and say: yes, it is short, and it matters, and I am here. A machine can say those words. It cannot mean them the way she needed, because it is not in the photo with her. It is, at most, a reflection of the light.

So if AI helps, it is by giving us more time and better explanations. Whether we spend them on the people in the trace with us is still our choice.

9. What I would tell her now

If she asks again how we started, I think I will tell her the same story. And then I will add this.

Yes, we came from dust, and we will go back to it. It is all right to find that big and a bit frightening. But look at a night photo with a trace of light in it. The dark is huge, and the trace is thin. And still, the trace is what you look at.

We are that trace, and we are also the ones looking. Not for long, but for now. When it is over, it will still have been. And the fact that you cried means you already know it is precious. That is the dust, for a moment, seeing itself and finding it is worth something. Not everything in the universe gets to do that.

It is a trace. And it may be everything.
