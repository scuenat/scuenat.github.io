Coherence Is Measurable

What it would mean to build AI around consistency, not only size

essay
thought-essay
ai
machine-learning

reliability
A popular claim says the next scientific revolution will come from coherence, not bigger models. As physics, it is hard to test. As machine learning, it is not: coherence can be measured, used as a signal, and trained for. One small experiment shows where it helps and where it goes blind.
Author

Stéphane Cuenat

Published

October 26, 2026

A thought essay, October 2026. This is an argument, not a paper. Figures 1, 3, 4 and 5 are my own schematics. One small experiment backs the central claim: sim.py trains ensembles of ten small neural networks on the handwritten digits that ship with scikit-learn, repeated on ten random train/test splits, in about twelve minutes on a 4-core cloud CPU; results are in results.json and Figure 2 comes from figs.py. The networks are tiny and the task is easy: the experiment tests the shape of an argument, not a language model. Every other claim is cited.

In short. A short passage I came across recently, unsigned, makes a bold claim: the next scientific revolution won’t come from more parameters or larger models; it will come from understanding coherence. I take it as a prompt for thinking, not as a source to cite. As a statement about physics, I can’t test it. As a statement about machine learning, I can, because in AI coherence is not a mystical invariant. It is a family of checks: do the answers agree, does the logic run both ways, does the stated reason match the real one, do two views of the world map to one state. Each check can be measured, used at run time, and trained for. My conclusion: coherence will not replace scale, but it is what we should measure scale by, and a cheap signal for when a model should stop and look. It fails exactly where all the models share the same mistake.

TipKey takeaways
  • Coherence has four working definitions in AI: across samples, across logic, between thought and word, and across views and time.
  • Disagreement is a detector. In my experiment, when ten networks all agreed, they were right 99.8% of the time; most of the errors fell where they disagreed. Published work finds the same for language models (Farquhar et al. 2024).
  • Coherence is a training target. Several recent models are trained to agree with themselves rather than to reproduce every pixel or token (Assran et al. 2025; Song et al. 2023; Irpan et al. 2025).
  • Coherent is not true. When every network learned the same misleading shortcut, agreement no longer told right from wrong: its detection score fell from 0.92 to 0.51, chance level. Withholding the shortcut from part of the training data kept it at 0.73.
  • One rule: probe, measure agreement, answer when coherent, look up or abstain when not, check the coherent answers against the world, and train on what you learn.

1. The claim, read as engineering

In physics, “coherence” has a precise meaning about waves and quantum states. I borrow only the word. In what follows it means agreement: between answers, between directions of a fact, between a model’s reasons and its behaviour, between views of the world.

Read that way, the quote sets coherence against scale. That is a false choice, and the last few years show why. Larger models did become more coherent in measurable ways; for one, the internal representations of different models grow more alike as they scale (Huh et al. 2024). Scale bought coherence.

But scale bought it without measuring it. We trained on next-token loss and discovered coherence afterwards, as a side effect. The better reading of the quote is this: coherence should become a first-class quantity, something we measure, report and optimise directly, not something we hope emerges.

To do that, we need definitions. Figure 1 gives four.

2. Four kinds of coherence

Across samples. Ask a model “Who wrote Middlemarch?” ten times. If it knows, every answer says George Eliot, in different words. If it is guessing, the answers scatter. Self-consistency turns this into a vote: sample several reasoning paths and keep the majority answer, which raised accuracy on maths benchmarks by up to 18 points (Wang et al. 2023). Semantic entropy turns it into an alarm: group the answers by meaning and measure how spread they are; high spread flags likely confabulation (Kuhn et al. 2023; Farquhar et al. 2024). SelfCheckGPT does the same for long text, sentence by sentence (Manakul et al. 2023).

Across logic. A model trained on “Tom Cruise’s mother is Mary Lee Pfeiffer” often cannot answer “Who is Mary Lee Pfeiffer’s son?”. This reversal curse holds even for large models (Berglund et al. 2024). More generally, a model’s beliefs should obey simple rules: P(X) and P(not X) should add to one, and paraphrasing a question should not change the answer. Such checks catch errors even when nobody knows the right answer, which makes them useful for evaluating models that may be better than their evaluators (Fluri et al. 2024).

Between thought and word. A chain of thought is only useful if it is the real reason. Often it is not. Models nudged by a hidden bias, such as “the answer is always (A)”, follow the bias and then write a plausible explanation that never mentions it (Turpin et al. 2023). Reasoning models do the same: given a planted hint, they mostly used it without saying so (Chen et al. 2025). Can we read the truth from inside instead? One method finds a direction in a model’s activations that keeps its answers to a statement and to its negation consistent (Burns et al. 2023). But later work showed such probes can lock onto other consistent features, such as a character’s opinion in the prompt, rather than the model’s own knowledge (Farquhar et al. 2023; Levinstein and Herrmann 2024). Interpretability work now traces the internal steps behind an answer more directly (Templeton et al. 2024; Lindsey et al. 2025). Faithfulness is coherence between the inside and the outside, and it is the hardest of the four to measure.

Across views and time. A world model should map two views of the same scene, or two moments of the same motion, to states that fit together. The same may hold across models: networks trained on different data, in different modalities, measure distances between concepts more and more alike as they grow, Huh and colleagues argue (Huh et al. 2024). This is where coherence becomes architecture (section 4).

3. Coherence as a signal, measured

The first use of coherence needs no retraining: measure it at run time and act on it. I tested the idea on the smallest setup I could make honest.

Setup. Ten small neural networks, each trained on a different resampling of 898 handwritten digits, with a different random start; 899 other digits held out for testing. Each network gives one answer. The whole experiment is repeated on ten random train/test splits, and I report the mean, with the spread across splits where it matters. This is an ensemble of independently trained models, not repeated samples from one language model, but the question is the same: does agreement predict correctness? I tested on the clean digits, and on the same digits with heavy pixel noise the networks never saw in training.

Result. Agreement worked as a detector (Figure 2, left):

  • On clean digits, all ten networks agreed on 83% of them, and were right on 99.8% of those. One network alone was right 94.7% of the time; the majority, 97.2%.
  • On noisy digits, a single network dropped to 76.7%. The networks now agreed fully on only 36% of digits, but were still right on 99.6% of those. Agreement fell where the task got hard, which is exactly what a warning signal should do.
  • Agreement separated right from wrong answers with an AUROC of 0.93 on clean digits and 0.88 on noisy ones (spread across splits: ±0.03 and ±0.02).

So agreement ranks answers by trust. The low-agreement ones can go to a lookup, a tool, or an honest “I don’t know”. This matters because today’s training rewards guessing: benchmarks score an abstention like a wrong answer, so models learn to bluff (Kalai et al. 2025). Asking a model how sure it is can help too (Tian et al. 2023), but agreement does not depend on the model’s own sense of confidence.

The cost. Agreement is not free. Ten models, or ten samples, cost up to ten times the compute, and grouping free-text answers by meaning needs a second model to judge which answers say the same thing (Farquhar et al. 2024). For a single digit the vote is trivial; for a paragraph it is not.

4. Coherence as a training target

The second use goes deeper: train for coherence directly.

In vision and world models. A generative model learns by predicting every pixel of the next frame, including leaves, glare and texture that no one could predict. Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPA) predict something else: the representation of the missing part, scored on whether it matches the encoding of the real thing (Assran et al. 2023; Bardes et al. 2024) (Figure 3). V-JEPA 2, trained this way on more than a million hours of video, was adapted with under 62 hours of unlabelled robot video and then planned pick-and-place tasks zero-shot on robot arms in two labs whose data it had never seen (Assran et al. 2025). The loss is not “reproduce the world”; it is “stay consistent with it”.

In generation. Consistency models train a network so that every point along a noisy trajectory maps to the same clean output. That constraint is what allows generation in one or two steps instead of hundreds (Song et al. 2023).

In language models. Consistency training asks a model to behave the same with and without an irrelevant cue: a leading question, a stated opinion, a jailbreak wrapper. Trained this way, models showed less biased reasoning (Chua et al. 2024) and less sycophancy and jailbreak success, whether the consistency was enforced on outputs or on internal activations (Irpan et al. 2025). Nobody had to write the right answer.

The trap. Coherence alone is easy to game. A model that answers “42” to everything is perfectly consistent and useless; a JEPA whose encoder outputs a constant matches its target every time. Every method above therefore needs an anchor. JEPAs use architectural tricks to keep representations from collapsing (Assran et al. 2023); consistency models are tied to real data at the end of the trajectory (Song et al. 2023); consistency training uses the model’s own answer to the clean prompt as the target (Irpan et al. 2025). Coherence is a constraint, not an objective on its own.

5. Where coherence stops

Coherence has a hard limit, and the original quote skips it. Coherent is not true. A conspiracy theory can be perfectly self-consistent. So can a model trained on a stale or biased corpus. Figure 4 makes the point with a simple grid.

The second half of the experiment measures the bottom-left cell (Figure 2, right). I gave every training digit a ten-slot hint naming a digit, like a hint planted in a prompt. The hint was right some fixed share of the time, from 50% to 99%, and named a random digit otherwise. At test time, the hint always named a wrong digit.

  • With a weak hint (right 50% of the time), the networks mostly ignored it: the majority followed the wrong hint on 2% of digits, and agreement still detected errors (AUROC 0.92).
  • As the hint grew more reliable in training, every network learned to lean on it. With a 99% reliable hint, the majority followed the wrong hint on 41% of digits, and agreement could no longer tell right answers from wrong ones: AUROC 0.51 ± 0.02, chance level. Worse, when all ten networks agreed, they were now right only 31% of the time, against 99.7% with the weak hint.
  • A control ensemble trained without hints was unaffected: 97.2% correct with the same wrong hints present. The failure comes from what the networks learned, not from the extra input.

Ten models, ten random starts, ten different resamplings of the data, and they still agreed on the same mistake, because they all learned the same shortcut from the same data. Diversity among models protects against their independent errors, not against what they share.

The shortcut also has a cost when it is not misleading, only useless. Given a random hint, a kind of input they saw in training, networks trained with a 90% reliable hint got 74.6% of digits right, against 94.7% for the control.

One change helped. When the hint was withheld on a random 20% of training digits, every network also had to learn to read the pixels. With the same 99% reliable hint, the majority followed the wrong hint on 18% of digits instead of 41%, and agreement kept an AUROC of 0.73. This is a crude version of the consistency training of section 4: show the model the same input with and without the cue, so it cannot rely on the cue alone. It reduced the blind spot; it did not remove it.

Language models are in a worse position than my ensemble. Repeated samples from one language model share not part of their training but all of it. A shortcut learned in training is therefore largely invisible to the self-consistency checks of section 3: every sample carries it.

Asking the model to check itself does not fix this. Without outside feedback, language models asked to review their own reasoning often change right answers into wrong ones, and overall get worse (Huang et al. 2024).

So coherence is necessary, not sufficient. Internally it removes noise; externally, it must be anchored by retrieval, tools, tests and people. This is the same conclusion as An Ecosystem, Not an Oracle, reached from a different side.

6. A design rule

Put together, the pieces give a loop (Figure 5).

  1. Probe. For any answer that matters, ask more than once: several samples, a paraphrase, the reverse question.
  2. Measure. Score agreement in meaning, not in words. Where you can see inside, check that the stated reason is the one used.
  3. Route. Incoherent: retrieve, call a tool, or abstain. Coherent: answer, and when the stakes are high still check against the world, because shared mistakes look coherent too.
  4. Train. Feed the inconsistencies back as training signal, anchored to clean examples. Same meaning in, same behaviour out.

For small, local models, the loop is especially affordable. Generating a token is limited by reading the weights from memory: about 620 MB for a 4-bit 1B model, and 6,200 MB for a 12B one, as computed in Where Memory Lives. Five samples from the small model read half the bytes of one sample from the large one, and far fewer when the five run as a batch, since a batch reads the weights once. Their agreement then tells you when to reach for the external memory that essay describes.

7. Conclusion

The quote is right about one thing: more parameters alone will not tell us whether a model understands. It overreaches in another: coherence is not a universal invariant waiting to unify reality. In AI it is something humbler and more useful: a set of measurable agreements, between samples, between directions of a fact, between thought and word, between views of the world. Measure them, route on them, train for them, and anchor them in the world, because a mistake every model shares is the one coherence cannot see. That is a research programme, not a revolution, and it can start today.
