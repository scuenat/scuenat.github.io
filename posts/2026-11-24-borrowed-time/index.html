Borrowed Time

Time made a mind that learns on twenty watts. Our machines borrow that time from everything we ever wrote. What are we buying with it, and is there another way?

Essay, November 2026. A short essay about us and the machines we are building. Where I give a fact, I give the source; where I borrow an idea from physics, I say how far the comparison goes. The figures are drawings of my own, the second after a known result from physics. The code that draws them is linked under each one.

Updated on November 25, 2026. Section 5 now tells how it began: within days of the announcement, a mathematician in Arizona, helped by AI, posted a readable manuscript of the proof, close to 300 pages and still unfinished.

In short. A mathematician and an AI have proved the Riemann hypothesis, as reported, and a computer has checked every step. Written out, the proof would run past 250,000 pages, and nobody can read it yet. That raises questions worth asking. Is searching a way of thinking? Maybe understanding is search, paid for in advance. Our brain paid with time, and now learns all its life on about twenty watts. Our machines pay with time borrowed from everything people ever wrote, and with power. Machines are already helping to explain it: within days, a mathematician posted close to 300 readable pages, written with their help and not yet finished. But every mind pays for its own understanding, and it pays at human speed. So I propose another view of progress. No proof without a reader. Big models that explore, and small ones, close to us, that teach. And time to understand.

1. What is a proof for?

On 20 November 2026, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, announced that he and Claude, the company’s AI, had proved the Riemann hypothesis (Scientific American 2026). It is a question about the prime numbers, open for 167 years. The proof is written in Lean, a language in which a computer checks every step. The next day, as reported, the Lean and Mathlib communities certified that it is correct (Technology.org 2026). Written out in prose, it ran past 250,000 pages, and he chose not to post it (OfficeChai 2026). I wrote about that day in Long First, Short Later.

Read one page a minute, eight hours a day, and you would need a year and a half. So here is a truth, perhaps the most wanted in mathematics, in a book that no person will read. Even the certificate says that mathematicians still have work to do to explain the proof’s ideas. The journal Science put the question in its headline: can humans understand the answer (Science 2026)?

So, what is a proof for? To know that something is true, or to understand why? Today we have the first. Not yet the second.

In 1953, the philosopher Martin Heidegger closed a lecture on technology with a line I keep coming back to. “Questioning,” he wrote, “is the piety of thought” (Heidegger 1977). Piety here means a kind of respect. He asked us neither to reject technology nor to follow it blindly, but to keep questioning it. So this essay is a series of questions, each with a proposal. Not verdicts.

2. Is searching a way of thinking?

It is tempting to say that the machine only searches, so fast that it looks like thinking. I am no longer sure that is enough. These systems now find things nobody had found (Partly Wrong, Mostly Right). And what is a discovery, if not a search that ends somewhere new? In 1976, Allen Newell and Herbert Simon, two founders of the field, proposed that intelligence is search, guided by good rules of thumb (Newell and Simon 1976). If so, the question is not whether a machine searches. It is how the search was paid for.

Chess gives a clue. In 1997, Deep Blue beat the world champion by examining well over a hundred million positions every second (Campbell et al. 2002). Decades earlier, the psychologist Adriaan de Groot had asked strong players to think aloud. The grandmasters did not look at more moves than weaker players. They looked at a handful, and saw the good ones first (de Groot 1965). Then, in 2017, AlphaZero beat the strongest chess program of the time while looking at about a thousand times fewer positions (Silver et al. 2018). It had not escaped the search. It had moved it: millions of games against itself, played before the match, in training.

Here is the view I propose. Understanding is search, paid for in advance. The grandmaster paid with years of games. AlphaZero paid in a data centre. We could never try everything, so we had to understand.

That is where understanding starts, not where it ends. Paid-for search gives a feel for the right move. AlphaZero has that feel, but it cannot tell you why a move is good. A grandmaster can. Understanding why is that feel put into words that someone else can follow.

3. What made us?

Nobody designed the human brain. Selection did: hundreds of millions of years of small changes, most of them failures. It was not cheap. Evolution ran on the sunlight of the whole living world, through billions of lives. But the cost was spread so thin, over so much time, that each step was almost free. Then each of us added a childhood.

What it left is small. Everything we inherit at birth is written in about three billion letters of DNA (Nurk et al. 2022), less than a gigabyte by my count. A child learns language from fewer than a hundred million words heard by the age of thirteen, by researchers’ estimates (Warstadt et al. 2023). And the brain does all this on about twenty watts, a small light bulb (Raichle and Gusnard 2002; Balasubramanian 2021). It does not only think on twenty watts. It learns on twenty watts, every day of its life.

The researcher François Chollet proposed measuring intelligence just this way. Not by what a system can do, but by how little experience it needs to learn it (Chollet 2019). What if we measured our machines like that too? By that measure, the child is still far ahead. That seems to me a goal worth aiming at.

4. What if data is time?

Time made us in three ways. The time evolution took to design us, the time a childhood takes to fill a brain, and the slowness of thinking itself. Each time, nature spread its energy thin over time, instead of spending it all at once. We want the same mind without the wait. But I don’t think time vanishes when we skip it. We pay for it in another currency.

Look at training data as time. Llama 3, an open model from 2024, was trained on more than fifteen trillion tokens, the small pieces of words these models read (Meta 2024). By my own count, that is roughly a hundred thousand times what a child hears by thirteen. Every page of it was written by a person, over centuries. So we did not really skip the time. We borrowed it. Data is time, stored. That is not a reproach. It is a gift from everyone who ever wrote, and it deserves to be used well.

Then we add power. The International Energy Agency estimates that data centres used about one and a half percent of the world’s electricity in 2024. It expects that to more than double by 2030, mostly because of AI (International Energy Agency 2025). I must be careful here. One trained model is shared by millions of people, so counted per answer, it can be cheap. The point is that building these minds fast gathers, in one place, what nature spread over ages.

Physics has a name for this trade. A process run very slowly can waste almost nothing; run it faster, and it wastes more. In 1975, Frank Curzon and Boris Ahlborn showed what this means for an engine. Run it for the most power, and it is much less efficient than it could be. Push it faster still, and you get less power, not more (Curzon and Ahlborn 1975).

Chips sit on the fast side. They switch billions of times a second, and switching faster costs more energy (Horowitz 2014). The brain took the other side. On average, a neuron fires only a few times a second (Attwell and Laughlin 2001). It is slow and parallel, and it keeps its memory next to its computing. A chip does not, and fetching a number from memory costs it over a hundred times the energy of a multiplication (Horowitz 2014).

So nature paid in time, and we pay in borrowed time and power. Neither is free. The question is which we can afford, and for what.

5. What do we gain by going faster?

If we could shrink that time to almost nothing, what would we really gain, as human beings? Often, a lot. Innovation is not the enemy, and physics agrees: an engine run infinitely slowly gives no power at all. When a disease spreads, every month saved is lives saved.

But past a certain speed, an engine gives less. An engine is not a society, and I use it as an image, not a proof. Still, it invites a question. Could we go past that speed too?

Look at the proof again. Other models can read the 250,000 pages, find the steps that matter, write a shorter proof, and explain it as many times as we ask. The prime number theorem waited 84 years for its four-page proof. This time, the work began within days.

Jimmie Adriazola, a mathematician at Arizona State University, has posted a manuscript of the proof that a mathematician can read from beginning to end, without Lean. By 25 November it ran to 297 pages (Adriazola 2026). He used Claude, GPT and Kimi, three AI models, to help him. He started from the last line of the Lean proof and walked back through everything it depends on. In one part, he found that 631 theorems in Lean came down to eight statements. He is careful about what he has. The manuscript is a work in progress. It still lists steps that it states but does not yet prove, and specialists have not yet checked it in depth. He is not a number theorist, he writes, and he has learned a lot along the way.

Two things strike me. The first is the direction. AI helped to find the proof, and now AI is helping a person to understand it. The second is the length. An earlier version of his manuscript had 66 pages. It grew as he opened each step that the shorter version had only summed up. Understanding did not make the proof shorter, at least not at first. It made it longer, and readable.

But here the idea from chess comes back. If understanding is search paid for in advance, someone has to pay. The machine has paid for its own understanding of the proof, and it can even put it into words. That does not pay for ours. Every mind pays for its own understanding. A good explanation lowers the price, sometimes by a lot. But nobody can pay it for us, and we pay at human speed. The manuscript from Arizona shows both halves. The machines helped with much of the digging. He still had to learn the mathematics himself, one step at a time, and so will everyone who reads him.

So the slow part moves. Producing explanations used to be slow. Now, taking them in is. A thousand clear explanations a day help nobody if nobody has the time to read one. And we are not always good judges of our own speed. In one small experiment in 2025, experienced programmers using AI tools took 19 percent longer, yet felt about 20 percent faster (Becker et al. 2025). A good reason to measure, rather than assume.

6. Which AI do we want beside us?

I am not against AI. I use it in my work every day. So my question is not “big or small?” It is this one: which AI lowers the price of understanding, for one person?

That is what a good tutor does. It knows what you already understand, notices where you are lost, and waits. In 1984, the educational researcher Benjamin Bloom reported that the average student taught one to one did better than about ninety-eight percent of a class (Bloom 1984). Later reviews found a smaller gain, but still a large one, and found that good computer tutors came close to human ones (VanLehn 2011).

I believe a small brain, close to us, could be that tutor. It does not need to know everything. It needs to know one person well, and stay with them. On their phone or their desk, it would learn where they live, the way a brain does. I sketched such a local mind in An Ecosystem, Not an Oracle and Fast AI. The big and the small need not compete. One can be the explorer. The other can be the guide who walks home with you.

7. Three proposals

So, instead of a verdict, three proposals.

No proof without a reader. When a machine finds a result, let us call the work finished only when at least one person can explain it in their own words. Count understanding, not only answers. The first readers of this proof are already at work.

Let the big models explore, and the small ones teach. Build both, for different jobs. The frontier finds and shortens. The companion close to us helps one person understand, at their pace.

Give understanding its own time. For every answer a machine gives us quickly, keep some time to take it in: to read, to doubt, to explain it back. That time is not lost. It is how borrowed time gets repaid.

More than a century ago, the poet Rainer Maria Rilke wrote to a young man who wanted answers. He told him to be patient with what is still unsolved, and to “live the questions now”. Perhaps then, he wrote, one day you will live your way into the answer (Rilke 1934).

Time made a mind that learns on twenty watts. We are building minds on borrowed time. I don’t know the right speed. But I think it is a question worth living, not a race worth winning.
