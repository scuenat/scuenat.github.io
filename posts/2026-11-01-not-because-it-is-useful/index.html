Not Because It Is Useful

AI can follow the scientific method better than we can. What moved science was never only the method, and never only the answer

essay
ai
science
philosophy
The method checks ideas; it does not find them, and it does not want them. Poincaré and McGilchrist on what moves discovery, and what AI changes.
Author

Stéphane Cuenat

Published

November 1, 2026

Essay, November 2026. A short, philosophical essay. It starts from two passages: Henri Poincaré on why a scientist studies nature (Poincaré 1914), and Iain McGilchrist’s argument that great discoveries are not made by following the scientific method (McGilchrist and Adelson 2026). It asks what both mean now that machines can follow the method better than we do. It continues The Joy of the Question, this time about science itself.

In short. We tend to describe science by its method: hypotheses, tests, rules that keep us honest. The method is how we check an idea. It is not how we find one, and it is not why we look. Poincaré said the scientist studies nature not because it is useful but because it is beautiful, with the beauty of an order the mind can grasp. McGilchrist argues that the great breakthroughs came from breaking the rules and seeing the whole, not from following the procedure part by part. AI is the method made into a machine, and it can even propose ideas, as long as they are plausible ones. It can hand us solutions. It cannot hand us the reason we wanted them, or the joy of grasping why they are true, because neither can be delegated. If we let usefulness become the only measure of science, AI will win, and we will have forgotten what science was for. If we let the machine carry the method, we may have more room than ever for what lies beyond it.

1. Two passages

Two passages started this essay.

The first is more than a century old. “The scientist does not study nature because it is useful to do so,” Poincaré wrote. “He studies it because he takes pleasure in it, and he takes pleasure in it because it is beautiful.” He added at once that he did not mean the beauty that strikes the senses, but “that more intimate beauty which comes from the harmonious order of its parts, and which a pure intelligence can grasp” (Poincaré 1914, bk. I, ch. 1).

The second is recent. In a conversation about science, the psychiatrist and philosopher Iain McGilchrist argues that great discoveries are not made by following the scientific method (McGilchrist and Adelson 2026). As I read him, the breakthroughs that changed how we see the world came from people who broke the rules rather than applied them, and who saw things whole and connected rather than only taking them apart.

The two passages are not the same claim, but they meet at the same point. Poincaré speaks of motive: what makes someone look. McGilchrist speaks of manner: how the looking that matters is done. Neither is about the method. And the method is exactly what we have now taught machines to do.

2. What the method does, and what it does not

It helps to be precise about what the scientific method is for. The philosopher Hans Reichenbach drew a famous line, much debated since: the context of discovery, how an idea comes to someone, and the context of justification, how it is tested and defended once it is there (Reichenbach 1938). The method lives almost entirely on the second side. It tells us how to check an idea, how to rule out error, how to make a result something others can trust. It says very little about where ideas come from. Kuhn and others have argued that the two sides cannot be cleanly separated, and they are right that the line is blurred. But it remains a useful way to see what the method was built for.

This is not a weakness. Justification is what makes science public. Anyone, in principle, can repeat a test. Discovery is private, unruly and hard to teach. That is why textbooks present science as method: the method is the part that can be written down.

But the part that can be written down was never the whole. Thomas Kuhn showed that normal science solves puzzles inside a frame, and that the frame itself changes in ways its own rules cannot justify (Kuhn 1962). Paul Feyerabend went further and argued that no single method runs through the history of science, and that the great advances often broke whatever rule was in force (Feyerabend 1975). Michael Polanyi argued that all knowing rests on personal commitment, and that “intellectual passions” decide which problems are worth a life (Polanyi 1958).

McGilchrist adds a sharper point. The method works by analysis: it divides a problem into parts and examines each. That is powerful, and it is also a way of attending to the world that can lose sight of the whole. A breakthrough is often a new whole: a pattern that makes many parts suddenly fit. You do not reach it by examining the parts more carefully. You reach it by seeing differently.

3. Not because it is useful

Poincaré’s sentence is easy to read as a pleasant sentiment. I think it is a claim about what science is.

If the scientist studied nature because it is useful, science would be a means: we would want the answer for what it lets us do, and any way of getting the answer would be as good as any other. Poincaré says the opposite. The scientist wants to grasp something: the order among the parts. And grasping is not a result you can be handed. It happens in you, or not at all.

This is older than Poincaré. In Plato’s Theaetetus, Socrates tells a puzzled young mathematician that his wonder is the mark of a philosopher, because philosophy begins in wonder (Theaetetus 155d). Wonder is not a wish for a solution. It is the feeling that something is more than we understand, and that understanding it would be worth a great deal, whatever it is good for.

Kepler is my favourite example of how this works, and of its risks. He was driven by the conviction that the heavens were built on harmony. His first theory nested the planets’ orbits inside the five perfect solids of geometry, and it was wrong. But the same search for harmony kept him at Tycho Brahe’s measurements for years, through failure after failure, until he found that the orbits were ellipses. In a book called Harmonices Mundi, the harmony of the world, he published the law that links each planet’s year to its distance from the sun (Kepler 1619). What moved him was beauty. What decided was the data. He needed both.

So what motivates discovery is more than finding a solution. A solution ends a search. Understanding, the grasp of why it must be so, is what the searcher wanted all along. And the search itself, with its failures, its surprises and its pleasure, is not only a cost paid for the answer. For the people who do science, it is much of the point.

4. The method, made into a machine

Now consider what AI is. A large model learns from what has been written. It is extraordinarily good at the part of science that can be written down: recalling what is known, checking an argument, searching a space of options faster than any person. In that sense AI is the method made into a machine, and in some fields it is already a better practitioner of the method than we are.

It also reaches into discovery, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. A model proposes hypotheses, suggests experiments, recombines ideas from distant fields. But it proposes by plausibility: what is likely, given what has been written. That is discovery done the way the method would do it, if the method could dream. What is missing is not the ability to propose. It is the reason to care which proposal is true.

One could also object that AI is not reductive at all. It connects ideas across every field it has read, which sounds closer to McGilchrist’s whole than to the analyst’s parts. I take this seriously, and I do not think it can be settled today. A model sees the world through our data and our descriptions; we see it through our senses and our concepts, which are not free of filters either. Whether a machine can attend to the whole in McGilchrist’s sense, or only to the patterns in what we have recorded, is an open question, and I will not pretend to answer it.

What we do know is simpler. A model can hand us a solution. It does not want the solution, as far as anyone can tell, and it does not find the harmony of nature beautiful. And even if a future machine did, its wonder would not be ours. The answer can be delegated. The grasp cannot. If I read a proof I did not find, I may come to understand it, but only by walking through it myself. No one can understand something on my behalf.

5. What AI changes in us

So the question is not whether AI will do science. It will do more and more of the part we called the method. The question is what happens to the part that was never the method: the wonder, the taste, the wish to understand.

I see a real risk. When answers become cheap, it becomes tempting to value science only for its answers, that is, for its usefulness. Usefulness is not a bad reason to value science. Most of us are grateful for science because it cures, feeds and connects us, and that is a good reason to fund it. But it is not why anyone devotes a life to it, and it is not what makes a result understood. Treating it as the only reason is precisely the view Poincaré rejected. If science is only a way of producing useful results, then a machine that produces them faster is simply better, and the people who used to do science have nothing left to do but consume. We would not lose science. We would lose the reason anyone loved it.

There is a subtler risk too. Wonder and taste are not given. They grow through the search: through trying, failing, and slowly seeing why. A generation that receives answers without searching may keep the answers and lose the capacity to wonder at them.

And there is a real opportunity. For most of history, the method was expensive. Scientists spent most of their time checking, calculating and defending, and only a little of it wondering. If machines carry much of that weight, we could have more time than ever for what lies beyond the method: for asking why the world is ordered the way it is, for seeing things whole, for the pleasure Poincaré described. AI could return us to the reason we started. It will do so only if we choose it, because the method, left to itself, will always choose the next useful step.

6. The case against beauty

I should give the strongest objection its due. Beauty misleads. Kepler’s perfect solids were beautiful and false. In our own time, the physicist Sabine Hossenfelder has argued that the search for elegant theories has led parts of physics astray for decades, toward ideas that are beautiful and untested (Hossenfelder 2018). And most of science, honestly, is not wonder at all. It is careful, patient checking, and it is that checking that turns a beautiful idea into knowledge.

I agree with all of this, and I do not think it weakens the argument. Poincaré never claimed that beauty proves anything. Beauty proposes; the method disposes. A science of beauty without method is fantasy. A science of method without beauty is a procedure with no reason to run. What Poincaré and McGilchrist remind us is that the second danger is the one we are now drifting toward, because the method is the part we have learned to automate.

7. Why we look

When we ask what AI means for science, we usually ask what it can find. I think the better question is what it leaves us to look for, and why.

The machine can follow the method, perhaps better than we ever did. It can give us solutions, many of them useful. What it cannot give us is the wonder that made us ask, or the joy of seeing for ourselves why the answer must be so. Those were never part of the method. They were the reason for it.

The scientist, Poincaré wrote, does not study nature because it is useful. In an age of useful machines, that may be the most important sentence about science we have.
