Stop Reviewing Pull Requests

A provocative position paper: approve what you cannot undo, watch everything else

paper
position
ai
agents
programming
A deliberately provocative position paper. Per-change human code review was the right control when code was written by hand and late fixes were expensive. With agentic coding, a simple cost model and a simulation suggest a different rule: a person approves only what cannot be undone, through a thin gate at the point of no return, and everything else merges under rules and is watched by a daily audit of the whole codebase. The paper states the conditions under which the rule holds, the predictions that would prove it wrong, and a protocol to test them.

A provocative paper, on purpose. A position paper in the form of a short scientific paper: a thesis, a model, a simulation, and predictions that could prove it wrong. It follows Review the Intent, Not the Diff, which kept review and changed its object; this paper asks whether we need the review at all. Figures are my own, drawn and simulated with the code in figs.py.

Abstract. Human approval of every pull request became standard when code was written by hand and defects found late were expensive. I model the review gate as an investment that pays when p \cdot d \cdot \Delta C > r: the chance a change still carries a defect after automated checks, times the share a reviewer catches, times the late-fix cost avoided, must exceed the cost of the gate. Agentic coding shrinks the late-fix cost of most changes, but not where harm survives a revert. The model therefore yields a rule rather than a ban: approve what you cannot undo; watch everything else. Watching means rules in every session and a daily audit of the whole codebase. The approval that remains is a thin gate placed late, at the point of no return, rather than at the merge. In a simulation of a team merging 40 changes a day, the audit and the thin gate together leave a third of today’s latent defects and the same number of irreversible incidents, for about 80 minutes of people time a day instead of 16 hours. I state the conditions under which this holds and five predictions to test it.

TipClaims
  1. The rule. Approve what you cannot undo. Watch everything else.
  2. Why. A gate pays only when p \cdot d \cdot \Delta C > r. Cheap fixes and fast rollback make \Delta C small, except where harm survives a revert.
  3. Watching. Rules every session must follow, merge on green, and a daily audit whose report drives the team’s work.
  4. The gate that remains. Thin and late: at the point of no return, a few questions with evidence, rarely needed. It guards the act, not the code.
  5. The condition. Watching beats approving only if the audit reaches far enough and fast enough: c > d \, (1 + h_{\text{users}} / h_{\text{audit}}).
  6. The test. Teams that adopt the rule should fail no more often and find defects sooner. If they do not, the thesis is wrong.

1. Introduction

For most changes, nobody should have to approve the pull request. The usual answer is that this would end in chaos. I do not think so, and the reason is economic. Review is a control, and every control has a price. We paid it because the gate was the cheapest place to catch a defect: minutes at review against days once it reached users.

Agentic coding changes the price on both sides. Rules can be enforced inside every session instead of at its end. A fix takes an agent minutes, and a flag can switch a change off in seconds. Correction is cheap now, and I believe that is what changes the whole picture. Human attention stays expensive, and the gate spends it on every change, whether or not that change needs it.

Correction is not cheap everywhere. Deleted data, leaked credentials or money sent to the wrong account cannot be taken back by a revert. Hence the rule: approve what you cannot undo; watch everything else. Where approval remains, it must come late and stay small, or it brings little.

2. Background: what review was for

Inspections. Formal code inspection began at IBM in the 1970s as a defect-removal step (Fagan 1976), justified by the claim that a defect costs one to two orders of magnitude more to fix in operation than in development (Boehm 1981). The empirical basis of that curve is thinner than its fame (Bossavit 2015), and agile practice argued that integration and automated tests flatten it (Beck 1999).

What modern review finds. Lightweight review replaced inspections in most teams (Rigby and Bird 2013). At Microsoft, developers named finding defects as their first motivation, yet most comments concerned code improvement and understanding (Bacchelli and Bird 2013); a later study was titled Code Reviews Do Not Find Bugs (Czerwonka et al. 2015). About three quarters of the issues found concern evolvability rather than behaviour (Mäntylä and Lassenius 2009; Beller et al. 2014). At Google, people valued education, norms, gatekeeping and accident prevention most (Sadowski, Söderberg, et al. 2018).

Evidence for review. Components with low review coverage had more post-release defects in three open-source projects (McIntosh et al. 2014), and careful reading improves quality (Kemerer and Paulk 2009). On approval, the largest survey of delivery performance found that external approval slowed delivery without reducing failed changes, and recommended lightweight peer review instead (Forsgren et al. 2018).

Agentic review today. The Claude Code team at Anthropic already lets low-risk changes merge without a person when automated review passes, and reports more merged changes and fewer reverts (Claude Code team, Anthropic 2026). The rule proposed here replaces their risk score with a simpler question and makes no-approval the default.

3. A cost model of the gate

Consider one change. Let p be the probability that it still carries a defect after all automated checks; d the share of such defects a human reviewer catches; \Delta C = C_{\text{late}} - C_{\text{review}} the cost avoided by catching it at review; and r the full cost of the gate for one change, including reading, waiting and context switches. The gate pays for itself when

p \, d \, \Delta C > r . \tag{1}

The late cost is the work of the fix plus the harm done while the defect lives. If harm accrues at a rate k and the defect is found after a time \tau,

C_{\text{late}} \approx c_{\text{fix}} + k \, \tau . \tag{2}

What agents change. Session rules can lower p: an agent can be made to add tests for every changed behaviour, keep coverage of changed lines above a threshold and follow a conventions file, and checks delivered while code is written are the ones developers act on (Sadowski, Aftandilian, et al. 2018). This is not automatic, since code written with AI assistants can be less secure (Pearce et al. 2022; Perry et al. 2023), so the simulation keeps p the same under every policy. Meanwhile r does not fall: careful review proceeds at a few hundred lines an hour (Kemerer and Paulk 2009), volume rises, and waiting grows roughly as \rho/(1-\rho) with reviewer utilisation \rho (Kingman 1961).

The main assumption. The term that moves most is \Delta C. Assumption A: for most defects, an agent can produce a correct fix within an hour of the report, and a flag or a rollback can stop the harm within minutes. Under A, c_{\text{fix}} is small, flags cap k (Humble and Farley 2010), and an audit caps \tau. Agents resolve a meaningful share of real repository issues under controlled evaluation (Jimenez et al. 2024), and the best teams in Forsgren et al. (2018) already restored service in under an hour; neither measures agent-made fixes in production, so prediction H3 tests A directly.

Where the rule comes from. Assumption A flattens the cost curve only for harm that a revert removes. When harm persists after a revert, k\tau stays large however quickly the defect is found, and the gate still pays (the red point in Figure 3). Everything else drifts below the break-even line. The model does not say “never review”; it says exactly where review still pays.

4. The rule in practice

What cannot be undone. A change is a one-way door (Bezos 2016) when its harm survives a revert: data destroyed or exposed, money moved, credentials or signing keys changed, a public interface released outside the team, a message sent to customers. The list is explicit, versioned and part of the session contract. Code that touches it merges like any other; its irreversible step waits at the thin gate (§5). Sessions steered from outside the team belong to the same category: an outsider’s issue or a web page can direct an agent (Greshake et al. 2023), and a planted backdoor or a leaked secret cannot be undone either. The contract records where each session’s instructions came from, so such sessions are flagged automatically.

What watching means. Every change merges on green, and everything that is not a one-way door is released without waiting. It is watched at three points (Table 1): before the merge by a session contract enforced by the agent and CI; after it by flags and monitoring; and once a day by an audit that reads the whole codebase and production signals and writes a short report that the team reads instead of forty diffs. When a problem keeps reappearing, the team turns it into a rule, so the contract gradually absorbs what reviewers used to write in comments.

What the audit sees that a pre-merge check cannot. Fast checks belong in the contract. The audit adds slow checks such as mutation testing and fuzzing, patterns across changes, production signals since yesterday’s merges, and trends people can judge before any rule encodes them. A per-change reviewer misses the second and third kind too.

The integrity rule stops the most common failure of coding agents, an agent that edits a test until it passes. Coverage is paired with mutation testing because coverage alone is a weak measure of test quality (Inozemtseva and Holmes 2014) and becomes a target once enforced (Strathern 1997); mutation testing measures whether tests notice a change in behaviour (Papadakis et al. 2019).

Who is accountable. The person who starts a session owns what it merges. Steering the agent and looking at its output is part of writing, and it stays. What disappears is the second person whose approval was required.

Intent changes. The previous essay sent every change of intent to a person before merging. The rule refines that: a change to a contract or invariant that can be reverted merges, but never silently. It updates its decision record, and the team reads it the next morning. Only intent that guards a one-way door meets the thin gate.

5. The thin gate

Where the remaining gate sits and how much it asks decide whether it is worth having. A gate placed early and made thick, with a person reading every diff of every risky change, brings little. A gate placed late and kept thin keeps most of the value that is left.

Late: at the point of no return, not at the merge. Merging is reversible; code that touches a one-way door is safe until it runs. The irreversible act comes later: running the migration, rotating the key, sending the message, turning on the flag that moves money. The gate belongs there. The person judges what will actually run, with real data and configuration; the approval cannot go stale; and the gate holds back one action, not the team.

Thin: a few questions with evidence, not a diff. Reading code is slow, and reviews get shallower as changes grow (Kemerer and Paulk 2009). At the point of no return, five questions suffice (Figure 4). What exactly cannot be undone? What did a rehearsal, such as a dry run on a copy of production, show? What is affected, and can the action be staged? What is the way back, and has it been tested? Who asked, and did any instruction come from outside the team? The agent prepares the answers; the person checks them and chooses to go, to stage the action, or to stop. Staged releases are standard practice when an effect is uncertain (Beyer et al. 2016).

In the terms of Equation 1, a thin, late gate raises d, because the person examines evidence about the one thing that matters, lowers r, because the decision takes minutes and is rarely needed, and sits where \Delta C is largest. A thick, early gate does the opposite on all three counts. One case needs more than five questions: a session steered from outside the team, where the danger hides in the code itself. There a person reads the diff or, better, the work is redone in a session the team steers.

Rare: keep it thin on purpose. Gates grow. Every incident adds an item to the list, and a long enough list turns the thin gate back into the old one, approved by tired people who no longer read. Track its load: the share of changes that reach it and the minutes per decision. When the same kind of action keeps arriving, make it undoable instead of adding approvers: soft deletes, schema changes that expand before they contract (Ambler and Sadalage 2006), keys that can be rolled back, releases region by region. Each piece of reversibility moves a class of changes from “approve” to “watch”. The best gate is one with less and less to do.

6. A simulation

I simulated a team under five policies: a gate on every change (today’s common practice), no gate and no audit, an audit alone, the audit with the thin gate (the proposal), and a gate on every change with the audit. Each change may carry a reversible defect; one in twenty also carries an irreversible step, which may itself be defective. A defective irreversible step that runs unstopped is an irreversible incident: the nightly audit comes too late for it. The parameters (Table 2) are chosen rather than measured and, where I had to choose, generous to review.

Chaos is real, but it is not the proposal. With no gate and no audit, latent defects double and irreversible incidents double. The critics are right about that policy, not about the alternative.

Each mechanism does one job. The audit alone cuts latent defects to a third of today’s, but leaves irreversible incidents at the chaos level, because a nightly audit finds them after the harm. The thin gate alone brings incidents back to today’s level, for 20 minutes a day. Together they beat today’s practice on reversible defects and match it on irreversible ones, at a twelfth of the people time. If the evidence at the thin gate lets it stop 80% of defective steps instead of 50%, incidents fall to 0.4 a month, well below today’s.

The price of the full gate. Adding a gate on every change to the audit lowers latent defects from 3.3 to 1.6 and does not reduce irreversible incidents, for 16 hours a day: about 10 engineer-hours for every day of latent defects it prevents (16 h ÷ 1.7 ≈ 9.4). Does a latent defect in this part of the system cost more than 10 engineer-hours a day? For a reversible defect in an internal tool, rarely. For a payment flow, easily, and that is a one-way door with its own thin gate.

Speed and reach of the watch. By Little’s law (Little 1961), latent defects equal the rate at which they enter times how long they stay. A gate removes at most the factor (1-d), here a half; an audit shortens the stay, here by a factor of six. But the audit may see only a share c of defects, the rest being found by users. The proposal then beats today’s practice when

c > d \left(1 + \frac{h_{\text{users}}}{h_{\text{audit}}}\right). \tag{3}

In plain words, the watch must see more kinds of defect than a reviewer would have caught, and the slower it is, the more it must see. With the values of Table 2, an audit that finds what it sees in about two days must see at least 60% of defects, and one that takes five days, 75% (Figure 6). If a team’s defects are mostly of a kind no machine can notice, the rule fails for that team, and Equation 3 says so before any harm is done. Measure c first, and where it falls below the bound, keep reviewing changes to that part of the code until the watch improves.

7. Predictions and a test protocol

Speed is not a useful test: removing a gate obviously speeds up merging. The thesis stands or falls on quality and on its assumptions. For teams that adopt the rule, compared with similar teams that keep the gate:

  • H1. No loss of stability. Change failure rate and time to restore service (Forsgren et al. 2018) are no worse, within a margin fixed in advance, and irreversible incidents do not rise.
  • H2. The watch sees far enough. The share of defects found first by the audit or monitoring rather than by users, an estimate of c, exceeds the bound of Equation 3, and defects are found sooner.
  • H3. Fixes are cheap (Assumption A). For defects found by the audit, the median time from report to merged fix is under one working hour, and a flag or rollback contains the harm before the fix lands.
  • H4. The report is used. At least half of the audit’s findings are fixed or explicitly dismissed within a week. An audit nobody reads turns the proposal into the chaos case.
  • H5. The gate stays thin. The share of changes reaching the thin gate and the minutes per decision stay small, and fall as more actions are made undoable.

Protocol. In a stepped-wedge design, teams adopt the rule one at a time, in random order, over several months, so every team is observed under both policies and calendar effects are controlled. H1 is a pre-registered non-inferiority test; H2 to H5 and lead time are secondary outcomes, measured from repository, pipeline and incident logs rather than self-reports, since teams cannot be blinded. If H1, H2 or H3 fails, the thesis is wrong for the teams studied.

8. Objections and limitations

“Review demonstrably reduces defects” (McIntosh et al. 2014). Compared with no review, on hand-written code, yes. The relevant comparison is review against watching, which H1 and H2 test. The recommendation of peer review in Forsgren et al. (2018) rests on data that predate agents; extending it is an extrapolation.

“Review is how teams share knowledge” (Bacchelli and Bird 2013; Sadowski, Söderberg, et al. 2018). It was. The replacement is deliberate: decision records, conventions, and a daily report read by the whole team rather than forty diffs each read by one person.

People adapt. Without a reviewer, authors may grow careless, or more careful because they alone own the result. The simulation holds p fixed; H1 measures the net effect.

Compliance. Some standards require custom code to be reviewed before release; PCI DSS sets conditions on who may review when the review is manual (PCI Security Standards Council 2022). Whether automated review satisfies them is for auditors to decide; where it does not, the code joins the list of one-way doors.

Loss of skill. Automation leaves people responsible for rare, hard cases while removing the practice that prepares them (Bainbridge 1983). Teams must keep the skill on purpose: reading the audit’s findings in the code, tracing incidents by hand, rotating who does it.

Limits of the model. It is linear and treats defects as independent. It does not weight defects by harm, so if reviewers mainly catch severe ones it is biased against the gate; the irreversible cases, which keep their gate, are meant to cover the worst. It counts the audit as an hour of reading, ignoring its compute cost and false alarms. It holds d constant, although reviewers tire and large changes get shallower reviews, and it ignores what reviewers learn. The thin gate’s catch rate is assumed equal to a reviewer’s, not measured. The parameters describe one team at one rate: the direction of the result holds wherever Equation 3 holds, not its magnitude. Finally, the paper assumes writing code is no longer the bottleneck, and the evidence on how much AI speeds up experienced developers is mixed (Becker et al. 2025).

9. Conclusion

The pull request review was the right control while late defects were expensive and a person’s reading was the main check. With agentic coding, rules run inside every session, most defects are cheap to fix the next day, and the one step that has not become faster is the person reading the diff.

What keeps a codebase healthy is how quickly defects are found and how cheaply they are undone, not whether someone looked at each change before it merged. Where harm cannot be undone, a person still decides, late and briefly, at the point of no return: the gate guards the act, not the code. Where a machine cannot see, the team should measure that before it lets go.

This means giving up control of each change. In return, we can simplify our processes instead of adding layers to protect a kind of control that, in the past, was the only kind available. Approve what you cannot undo. Watch everything else.
