Partly Wrong, Mostly Right

Seven months after my TEDx talk, machines prove theorems and solve physics problems. What I got wrong, and why innovation still needs us.

Seven months after my TEDx talk, AI proves theorems and computes physics no one could. I was partly wrong. Why the frontier still needs people.
Author

Stéphane Cuenat

Published

November 20, 2026

Essay, November 2026. A personal essay, written seven months after my TEDx talk in Delémont, The Impossible: A Springboard for Humans, a Frontier for AI. I look back at what I said, at what machines have done since in mathematics, physics and biology, and at what I got wrong. Several of these results come from company announcements or press reports; where I rely on someone’s account, I say whose. The first figure is a schematic of my own; the second places the results on a timeline, with the dates as reported. The code that draws them is linked under each one.

In short. In April, on a stage in Delémont, I said that the impossible is a springboard for humans and a frontier for AI. Seven months later, machines have disproved a conjecture of Erdős and found a new formula in particle physics. They have computed a result physicists could not reach. And they have produced a proof, checked by a computer, that a central theory of physics exists. I was wrong about two things. These systems do reach genuinely new places, not only recombine old ones. And for me, AI is no longer only a tool. But the core of what I said still holds. In every one of these stories, a person chose the question, and people had to decide what the answer means. Machines have become very good at finding answers. Choosing what is worth asking, and knowing when we have truly arrived, is still ours. I believe it will stay ours for a long time.

1. Watching myself again

A few evenings ago, I watched my TEDx talk again. Then I reread the essay I wrote alongside it, The Answer You Can’t Check. It made three points. First, large models are brilliant at recombining what already exists. At the edge of what we know, they don’t stop: they fill the gap with something that looks right. Second, at the frontier there is no oracle, nothing to check a new answer against. Third, what guides a person there is a kind of compass: the sense of which unknown is worth chasing, and the instinct that a beautiful answer is hollow.

And then this sentence: “For me, AI is a tool, and only a tool.” I ended with: “Keep the tool. Keep your head too.”

That was April. Now it is November. I want to be honest about what these seven months did to those words.

2. Seven months

Even before the talk, the signs were there. In 2025, machines reached gold-medal level at the International Mathematical Olympiad (Google DeepMind 2025; Hubert et al. 2025), and a coding agent from Google DeepMind found new algorithms that beat the best known ones (Novikov et al. 2025). But olympiad problems have known answers. Research problems do not. What changed in 2026 is that machines started to work on problems nobody had solved.

In mathematics, in May, a reasoning model from OpenAI disproved a conjecture that Paul Erdős made in 1946. It is about how many pairs of points in the plane can lie at the same distance. According to the company, outside mathematicians checked the proof (OpenAI 2026a). In October, the company released more than 700 research papers produced with its models. Among them was a proof of the “quasi-Riemann hypothesis”, a weaker cousin of the famous Riemann hypothesis, which, Quanta reports, experts have studied and take seriously (Quanta Magazine 2026). The mathematician Frank Calegari, in a guest post on Terence Tao’s blog, called it “a watershed in the history of mathematics”. He wrote that AI “is now undeniably capable of making groundbreaking contributions across many areas of mathematics” (Calegari 2026).

In physics, it went the same way. In February, a group of physicists, Andrew Strominger among them, looked at gluons, the particles that glue quarks together inside protons and neutrons. They showed that a certain kind of collision between gluons does not give zero, as many had presumed (Guevara et al. 2026). According to OpenAI, the key formula was first proposed by its model, then proved by another one, and checked by the physicists (OpenAI 2026b). In March, researchers at Google described a system that found exact formulas for the gravitational waves given off by cosmic strings, an open problem in theoretical physics (Brenner et al. 2026).

In September, two physicists at Anthropic asked Claude to compute a quantity in a simplified theory of particles, at nine “loops”, a measure of how deep the calculation goes. The human record was eight loops, set in 2023 by Lance Dixon and Andy Liu. Claude ran for days, as the post says, “without any scientific oversight more sophisticated than ‘keep going’”. Dixon checked the result (von Hippel 2026).

And this month, a committee appointed by OpenAI and chaired by the Nobel physicist David Gross confirmed a machine-made proof. It shows that Yang–Mills theory exists, one half of a Millennium Prize problem. The proof was checked in Lean, a program that verifies every step of a proof (Scientific American 2026; Science 2026).

Beyond physics and mathematics, it is the same story in biology. A multi-agent system called Robin proposed that ripasudil, an eye drop already used for glaucoma, could treat dry macular degeneration. It had never been proposed for that, and the team’s lab tests supported the idea (Ghareeb et al. 2025).

So, seven months. That is the world my April sentences now have to face.

3. What I got wrong

I want to start with what I got wrong, because it is the honest place to start.

First, “they only recombine.” I did hedge in April. I wrote that these systems can sometimes reach genuinely new points, and that I would not bet against them. But the heart of my argument was still the picture of a machine that recombines inside the known and guesses outside it. That picture is too small now. A formula for an amplitude that physicists presumed to be zero is not a recombination of the textbook. It goes against the textbook. A counterexample to a conjecture from 1946 is not an average of what was known. Whatever happens inside these models, the result lands somewhere new. I underestimated how often, and how soon.

Second, “only a tool.” This one is more personal. A tool waits for your hand. A hammer does nothing until you lift it. That is no longer how I work. In my daily work, AI agents now take whole tasks. They work through them and come back to me with a result. I wrote about this shift in my series on agentic coding. For my last experiment, an AI assistant helped me write the code, run the tests and write the plan. Without it, it would have taken months of evenings, not a few days (Two Cores and an Idea). Something that works for days while you sleep, on a problem you gave it, is not a tool in the sense I meant. It is closer to a colleague who never tires and does not always know when it is wrong.

So my April sentence needs a correction. AI is not only a tool. I was wrong on that.

4. The objection at full strength

Now, here is the strongest answer to the rest of my talk. I want to give it its full weight, because it is a serious one.

It goes like this. You said humans hold the frontier. But look at the list. A conjecture of Erdős, open for eighty years. A Millennium Prize problem, half solved. A physics calculation one loop beyond the best human record. These are not small tasks done faster. They are the frontier itself. Calegari, a working mathematician, not a company, says AI makes “groundbreaking contributions”. So the frontier has moved past you.

And it goes further. You said the real danger was the missing oracle: at the frontier, nothing can check a new answer. But the Yang–Mills proof was checked by a machine, step by step, in Lean. The oracle is being built. Soon, a machine will propose and a machine will check. Where, exactly, is the human?

I feel the force of this. Put that way, my talk looks like a line drawn in the sand just before the tide.

5. Look at who did what

But then I did what I always try to do with a big claim. I looked closely at who did what, story by story. And a pattern appeared.

Every question came from a person. Erdős asked about distances between points in 1946. The Clay Mathematics Institute chose Yang–Mills as a Millennium problem, because generations of physicists had worked on it without success. The nine-loop calculation began as a challenge from a science writer, Matt von Hippel, on a record Lance Dixon had set. The Robin team chose the disease. The machines answered, and answered brilliantly. But they answered questions that people had chosen, often after decades of caring about them. That is exactly what I meant by a springboard. The impossible problem is where we jump from. In 2026, we got a partner that jumps further than we do. It still jumps from our board.

Every answer still needed people to say what it means. Here the stories are even clearer. After the nine-loop result, Dixon wrote: “We have all the tools to validate any candidate solution a machine would provide us” (von Hippel 2026). Read it twice. The oracle was not missing for that problem, because people had spent decades building it. The machine could go far because humans had already built the road to check it.

For Yang–Mills, the Lean check is real progress, and I am glad of it. But a proof checker answers one question only: does each step follow from the one before? It does not answer the other one: did we write down the right thing? Three physicists made this point in October, in a paper on formal proofs in physics. A checked proof, they wrote, “does not establish that its definitions faithfully capture the intended physics” (Krippendorf et al. 2026). As reported, the written Yang–Mills proof runs to more than a thousand pages and has not been released. And the harder half of the problem, the “mass gap”, is untouched (Science 2026). A proof that no person yet understands is a result. It is not yet knowledge, not for us.

Calegari, the same mathematician who called the papers a watershed, was just as clear about the rest. Despite “some tremendous ideas”, the papers “are atrociously written and violate our community expectations of attribution. Some of them are even wrong” (Calegari 2026). Even in mathematics, where checking is cleanest, people still had to sort the right from the wrong.

And there is a quieter result from physics that I keep coming back to. In 2025, researchers trained models on the orbits of planets. The models predicted the orbits very well. But when asked to use what they had learned on new physics tasks, they “consistently fail to apply Newtonian mechanics” (Vafa et al. 2025). They could predict where a planet goes, without finding the law that moves it. That is the old story of Kepler and Newton, told again with a machine. Prediction is not understanding. And for an innovation, the understanding is what you carry to the next problem.

So the frontier did move. It moved a lot, and faster than I said. But it did not disappear. It moved to the two ends of the chain: choosing the question, and saying what the answer means. That is the first figure of this essay, and it is the correction I would make to my talk.

6. Why I think it will last

Now, I have to be careful. “Humans are still needed” can become a game where you keep moving the line each time a machine crosses it. I don’t want to play that game. So let me say why I think these two ends will stay human for a long time, and what would change my mind.

Questions come from caring. A good question is not the hardest one. It is the one that matters to someone: because it is beautiful, because a patient is waiting, because a bridge must not fall. That caring comes from living in a world, with a body, with stakes, with other people. Machines can now help sort questions. But choosing which unknown is worth a life of work is, I believe, tied to being someone for whom things matter.

Meaning lives in a community. A result becomes knowledge when people understand it, trust it, and can build on it. The physicists writing about formal proofs warned that the speed AI brings “currently comes at the cost of trust, credit, and community” (Krippendorf et al. 2026). That is not a technical problem a bigger model will fix. It is about how people decide, together, what they know.

The oracle is something we built. In every story where a machine went far, people had built the way to check it: a proof checker, a known lower-loop result, a lab test, a referee. Machines can now use these checks very well. But building a new way of checking, for a new kind of question, is itself invention. It is the compass again, at a deeper level.

This is a bet, not a proof. Here is what would make me lose it. Imagine a machine that, unasked, proposes a question nobody was working on. Years later, the community agrees it opened a whole field. And the machine can explain, in words people understand, why that question mattered. If that happens, I will write another essay, and its title will be shorter: Wrong.

7. What I would say on that stage today

If I had the red circle under my feet again, I would change two sentences.

I would no longer say that AI is only a tool. It is more than that, and pretending otherwise would be its own kind of blindness. Its reach is real.

And I would change my last line. In April I said: “Keep the tool. Keep your head too.” Today I would say: it is no longer only a tool, so keep your head even more. Not out of fear. Because the head is where the questions come from, and the place where an answer becomes understanding.

The machine now jumps further than we do. Someone still has to choose where the springboard points, and tell, when it lands, whether it landed somewhere worth being.
