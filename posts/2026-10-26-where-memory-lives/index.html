Where Memory Lives

Four experiments on what a small model should remember, and what it should look up

essay
experiment
ai
local-ai
memory
I measured how many facts fit in the weights of a small network, what it costs to change one, how fast an external memory answers, and when the KV cache outweighs the model. The result is a design rule for small, local, fast AI.
Author

Stéphane Cuenat

Published

October 26, 2026

An experiment, October 2026. Every number in Experiments 1 to 4 comes from sim.py, plain NumPy on a 4-core cloud CPU, with results in results.json and figures from figs.py. Other people’s numbers are cited where they appear. The networks are deliberately tiny: they test the shape of an argument, not any product. This essay puts to the test two ideas from Fast AI and An Ecosystem, Not an Oracle: bandwidth, and memory outside the weights.

In short. A language model keeps what it knows in four places: its weights, its working memory (the KV cache), whatever it can look up, and the habits it has compiled. Each is paid for at a different moment, and for a small model on a laptop that decides the design. Four experiments led me to one rule: keep in the weights what is used on every token; look up everything else, and keep what you look up short.

TipKey takeaways
  • Weights are the densest memory, and the most expensive to read. My networks held up to 1.7 bits per parameter, and a model reads all of them for every token.
  • Weights are hard to edit. Fine-tuning on 150 changed or new facts never kept both the edits and the other facts. Rehearsing everything did, after 630,000 training examples. A table needed 150 writes.
  • Looking up is cheap; rereading what you looked up is not. An index searched four million facts in 0.5 ms. But three retrieved passages then cost a 1B model 29 MB on every generated token.
  • Small models hit the working-memory wall first. At 4-bit weights, the cache of Llama 3.2 1B outweighs its weights from about 19,000 tokens.
  • Knowledge has a long tail. Under Zipf’s law, each tenfold increase in facts held answers the same extra slice of questions. Weights cover the head; the tail belongs in a lookup.

1. The question

Debates about small language models usually ask how much of a large model fits on a laptop. That treats a model as one block of knowledge to compress. I think the better question is what should live where?

Generating a token on a laptop is limited by memory bandwidth, not arithmetic: the machine reads every weight, and the whole cache of the conversation, for every token (Alizadeh et al. 2023). So where knowledge lives decides what it costs.

2. Experiment 1: how many facts fit in the weights

Setup. A fact is a key (a random vector of 32 numbers, standing for a question) and a value, one of V = 256 answers: \log_2 256 = 8 bits. The memory is a two-layer network,

f_\theta(x) = W_2\,\mathrm{ReLU}(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2, \qquad |\theta| = 32h + h + 256h + 256,

with hidden width h \in \{8, 16, 32, 64\}: 2,568 to 18,752 parameters. Each network learned N facts, offering from half to four times as many bits as it has parameters, in 3,000 full-batch Adam steps, three seeds each. Following Allen-Zhu and Li (Allen-Zhu and Li 2025), I count the bits it holds as

B(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \max\!\Big(0,\; \log_2 V + \log_2 p_\theta(y_i \mid x_i)\Big),

what its prediction knows about each answer beyond a uniform guess.

Result. Below capacity, every network kept every bit. Near capacity it peaked at 1.40 bits per parameter for the smallest network, 1.53 for the next, and 1.68 and 1.67 for the two largest, with seeds never more than 0.04 apart. Past capacity it stored less, not more: about 1.4 bits per parameter, with accuracy down to a fifth or a third. Three times more training lifted the peak to 1.80 but barely changed the decline (1.52 to 1.55, 1.45 to 1.49), so the decline is mostly interference between facts, not too little training.

The number is old: Cover showed in 1965 that a perceptron stores about two bits per weight (Cover 1965), and Allen-Zhu and Li measured about two bits per parameter in transformers (Allen-Zhu and Li 2025). Weights are a very dense memory: an 8-bit fact costs about five parameters, under five bytes at 8-bit precision. Quantised to 4 bits after training, the precision most small models run at, their models lost more than half of it.

3. Experiment 2: changing one’s mind

Setup. A network of 18,752 parameters learned 1,500 facts perfectly. Then 75 facts changed and 75 new ones appeared. I updated it in two ways, each over 4 learning rates and 3 training lengths: fine-tuning on the 150 edits only, or rehearsing everything, all 1,575 facts. An external table got the same edits: overwrite 75 rows, add 75.

Result. Fine-tuning on the edits alone never got both right. The runs that learned 97% or more of the edits kept only 28% to 35% of the other facts; the best compromise kept 48% and learned 47%. Rehearsing everything got both to 100%, after 630,000 training examples, a cost that grows with everything the network knows. The table needed 150 writes and 0.04 ms.

Catastrophic interference is not new (McCloskey and Cohen 1989), and editing methods such as ROME and MEMIT do far better than plain fine-tuning (Meng et al. 2022, 2023), though models still degrade as edits accumulate (Gupta et al. 2024). The point is the contrast: in a table, an edit is local by construction; in weights, every fact shares every parameter. Contacts change, prices change, a client asks you to delete their data. A memory you can overwrite row by row is one you can keep true.

4. Experiment 3: looking it up

Setup. I stored ten thousand to four million facts as unit vectors of 256 numbers, clustered around 2,000 topics as real embeddings are. A question is a stored key plus noise, a paraphrase. Exact search compares it with every key, O(Nd). An inverted-file index (IVF) groups the keys into c = 4\sqrt{N} clusters and searches only the p nearest, O\big((c + pN/c)\,d\big), which grows like \sqrt{N} (Douze et al. 2024).

Result. Exact search took 29 ms over a million facts and 100 ms over four million. Probing 4 clusters took 0.25 and 0.5 ms, reading 5 and 11 MB. On harder questions, one cluster missed 5% to 20%, while four clusters matched exact search (95.5% against 96% at the noisiest level). My clustered keys flatter any index: on real embeddings, the same recall takes more clusters, and more time.

An external memory is less compact than weights: 1,024 bytes per fact here, or 8 to 64 bytes with compressed codes (Douze et al. 2024), against under five in the weights. But weights are read in full for every token. A lookup reads a few megabytes, only when a question needs it.

What a looked-up fact really costs

In retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al. 2020), what the index finds is pasted into the prompt. From then on it sits in the working memory and is reread for every generated token.

Each token of context costs this model 32 KB. Three passages of 300 tokens add 29 MB to every generated token, over five hundred times the search itself. That is still only 5% of the 620 MB of weights, and far less than a model ten times larger, which would read 6,200 MB per token to answer about 11 more points of questions (Section 6). A lookup is cheap only if what it brings back is short: a fact, not three pages.

That is why recent architectures put the lookup inside the model, where a fetched fact never becomes context. Meta’s memory layers read a few rows of a trainable table per token and beat dense models with more than twice the compute, most on factual questions (Berges et al. 2024). DeepSeek’s Engram adds a hashed n-gram table read in constant time (DeepSeek-AI 2026). Gemma 3n keeps per-layer embeddings on local storage (Google 2025).

5. Experiment 4: the working memory

Setup. The KV cache stores a key and a value per token, layer and key-value head:

\text{KV bytes per token} = 2 \cdot L \cdot n_{kv} \cdot d_h \cdot b.

Each new token reads the whole cache and all the weights, so the cache weighs as much as the weights at

T^{*} = \frac{|\theta| \cdot b_w}{2 \cdot L \cdot n_{kv} \cdot d_h \cdot b_{kv}}.

I timed one layer shaped like Llama 3.2 1B (Meta 2024) generating a token over caches of 1,000 to 131,000 tokens.

Result. The weights cost 3.6 ms per token at any context. Attention cost 0.3 ms at 1,000 tokens, matched the weights at about 15,000 and took 49 ms at 131,000. By bytes alone the crossover is 59,000; my NumPy attention is slower than a matrix product, so it came earlier.

The formula gives 76,000 tokens for Llama 3.2 1B in 16 bits. Quantise the weights to 4 bits and it falls to 19,000: a long document, or one agent loop with a few tool outputs. For Llama 3.1 70B it is 108,000. Shrinking the weights moves the bottleneck to the working memory. The remedies all keep that memory short: grouped-query attention (Ainslie et al. 2023), sliding windows on five of every six layers in Gemma 3 (Gemma Team 2025), 2-bit caches (Liu et al. 2024), which push the 1B crossover back to 151,000, and Cartridges, small caches trained offline that match in-context learning with 38.6 times less memory (Eyuboglu et al. 2026).

6. The arithmetic of the long tail

How much should stay in the weights? Questions are not uniform: models know popular facts far better than rare ones (Kandpal et al. 2023), and retrieval helps most on the rare ones (Mallen et al. 2023). If the k-th most asked of F facts is asked with probability proportional to k^{-s} (Zipf’s law), a memory holding the K most asked answers

\text{coverage}(K) = \frac{H_{K,s}}{H_{F,s}}, \qquad H_{n,s} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} k^{-s}.

For s = 1, H_n \approx \ln n + 0.577, so each tenfold step adds the same share,

\text{coverage}(10K) - \text{coverage}(K) \approx \frac{\ln 10}{\ln F + 0.577},

about 11 points with a billion facts.

A model ten times larger reads ten times more per token for 11 more points. An index ten times larger reads about three times more, once per question. The head belongs in the weights, where it is used constantly; the tail belongs in a lookup. Apple’s hierarchical memories make this split in pretraining: a 160M model fetching 18M parameters from a 4.6B memory bank matched a regular model over twice its size (Pouransari et al. 2026).

7. What it means for small models and Fast AI

  1. Keep in the weights what is used on every token: language, reasoning, tool use, the structure of a domain. Not the price list.
  2. Keep the working memory short: summaries rather than transcripts, a compressed cache, a window.
  3. Look up the long tail, and bring back little: the fact, not the page. Every row can be inspected, corrected and deleted.
  4. Compile what repeats, as in Fast AI: a verified answer, a small tree or an adapter, checked before the model is called.
  5. Consolidate slowly: what is looked up constantly may earn a place in a habit; what is wrong is deleted, in one line, not one training run.

Agent memory systems are building the top of this stack: MemGPT, now Letta, pages context like an operating system (Packer et al. 2023); Mem0 and A-MEM extract and link memories across sessions (Chhikara et al. 2025; Xu et al. 2025); Titans learns a memory at test time (Behrouz et al. 2024). A small model has the least room in its weights and the least bandwidth to spare, so it gains the most from putting each memory in the right place.

9. What this experiment does not show

  • My facts are random. Real knowledge has structure a model can compress, so weights may do better than my networks.
  • My networks are tiny: two layers, at most 19,000 parameters.
  • My questions are synthetic. Real retrieval must embed a question first; it can miss, or find the fact and see the model ignore it.
  • Plain fine-tuning is the weakest editor. Better methods narrow the gap, but still edit weights that every fact shares.
  • NumPy is not a serving engine, which is why Figure 6 also gives the byte count.
  • Zipf is a model. A specialist’s questions may have a shorter tail, which argues for specialists.

10. Conclusion

We say a model knows something as if knowledge lived in one place. It lives in four, at four prices: dense weights read on every token, a working memory that grows with every token, a bulky but cheap and correctable lookup, and habits that cost almost nothing once built. Large models can afford to blur them. Small ones cannot, and that is their advantage. Keep in the weights what is used on every token, keep the working memory short, look up the long tail, compile what repeats.
