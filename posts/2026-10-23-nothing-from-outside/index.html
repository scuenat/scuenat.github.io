Nothing From Outside, Everything From Inside

curiosity
physics

A closed universe seen from nowhere has one state. Put an observer inside and the possibilities come back. A second evening of curiosity about nothing, everything, and grains inside grains.
Author

Stéphane Cuenat

Published

October 23, 2026

Continued in What Survives Comparison, which asks what happens with two observers instead of one.

Two ideas that would not stay apart

In A Universe Without a Bottom I left two thoughts side by side. The first: maybe nothing creates everything. The second: maybe there are grains inside grains, with no final layer.

I treated them as separate questions. A few days later they started to look like one question. And the black hole formula, which I thought said there must be a floor, turned out to say something stranger.

I am still not a physicist. This is still curiosity. But I think I asked the earlier questions slightly wrong.

Nothing is not empty

In the last essay I called infinite temperature “the closest thing to nothing.” Every possibility equally likely. No preference, no structure.

That was half wrong. A state where every possibility is equally likely is not a state with zero information. It is the state with the most possibilities: maximum entropy. You cannot tell the possibilities apart, but they are all there.

This matters because of where the model points. For several years Susskind has argued that this exact system, at infinite temperature, describes de Sitter space: a universe with nothing in it except a horizon around the observer. An empty universe like this carries the largest entropy any region can have. Its horizon is covered with tiles, just like a black hole.

So the two images in my first essay may be the same image. “Nothing” and “a surface that knows everything” could be one thing, seen from different sides.

A universe with one state

Then I found a result that seemed to say the opposite.

Some universes are closed: travel in a straight line long enough and you come back to where you started. Our universe might be one of them. Over the last few years several groups calculated how many different states such a universe can be in, using the newest tools of quantum gravity. The answer kept coming out the same: one.

One state. Not a large number, not a small number. One. The whole universe, stars and galaxies included, would carry less information than a single coin toss. There is nothing to choose between, so there is nothing to know.

The physicists themselves did not like this. Our universe clearly has more than one possibility in it. I am writing this sentence and not another one. So what went wrong?

Someone has to be inside

The answer proposed by Harlow, Usatyuk and Zhao is simple to say. The calculation forgot the observer.

It described the universe as if from outside. But a closed universe has no outside. Nobody can stand there and look at it. The philosopher Thomas Nagel called the dream of perfectly objective knowledge the view from nowhere: the world seen from no particular place. Here the view from nowhere is not just hard to reach. It returns nothing.

So put an observer inside, treat the edge of that observer as a kind of boundary, and the many possibilities come back.

One warning, because the word is dangerous. “Observer” here does not mean a mind, a consciousness or a person. In these calculations an observer is any physical system with a limited number of internal states that can keep a record. A clock would do. A detector would do. Nothing mystical is happening. What changes is that there is now a reference point.

There is a catch, and I find it beautiful. How much of the universe the observer can describe depends on how much the observer can hold. A bigger observer, with more internal states, sees a richer and more accurate universe. A tiny one sees a blurry one.

So the information in the universe is not simply “out there.” It exists between the universe and something inside it. This is close to an idea Carlo Rovelli proposed in 1996, relational quantum mechanics: physical properties are not owned by things, they exist only relative to other things. The new calculations do not prove this view. But they point the same way.

Two kinds of nothing

Now put the two results next to each other.

  • No observer: one state. Zero alternatives. Nothing to know.
  • One observer at the centre: a horizon full of tiles. Every alternative equally likely. Everything possible, nothing distinguishable.

Both look featureless. Both feel like “nothing.” But they are featureless for opposite reasons. The first has no possibilities. The second has all of them, and no way to tell them apart.

Here is my step. Maybe these are not two different universes. Maybe they are the same universe, once without a reference point and once with one. Without one, the universe is nothing. With one, it is everything its horizon can hold.

This is less of a leap than I first thought. De Sitter space, taken as a whole, is itself a closed universe, so the one-state result should apply to it too. And in 2022, Chandrasekaran, Longo, Penington and Witten showed that the entropy of a de Sitter horizon only becomes well defined once an observer is included, and that empty de Sitter space is then the state of maximum entropy. As far as I know, nobody has put these results together in exactly this way. But the pieces fit.

Philosophers got here long before the equations. Hegel opened his Science of Logic (1812) with this very puzzle: pure being, with no determination at all, cannot be told apart from pure nothing. What sets things in motion, for Hegel, is becoming, the passage between the two. Two centuries later, physics seems to add one thing. What turns nothing into everything is a point of view.

Grains again

In the first essay I was stuck on a contradiction. History suggests the layers never end. The black hole formula says a region can hold only a limited amount of information, so it cannot hide infinite layers. There must be a floor.

Remember the 1987 bubble: a tiny pocket of dense energy that grows into a new universe. From outside it looks like a small black hole that disappears. From inside it is a whole cosmos.

Physicists have known for a long time that this is uncomfortable. A small black hole should hold only a little information. How can a whole universe fit inside? Wheeler called this kind of object a “bag of gold.”

The one-state result changes the question. At first, seen from outside, the bubble is a small black hole, and that black hole does carry some information on its surface. But the bubble then pinches off and becomes a separate closed universe. From that moment, with nothing of ours inside it, it counts as one state. It costs us almost nothing. From the inside, with its own observers, it is everything.

And the same flip can repeat:

  • Inside that grain, a new bubble forms. To the grain, it counts as one state. To its own inhabitants, it is a cosmos.
  • Our universe might be a grain in someone else’s. To them, we would be one state. To us, we are everything.

Leibniz imagined something like this three centuries ago. In his Monadology (1714) he wrote that each portion of matter is like a garden full of plants, and that each branch of every plant is again such a garden. He also said that his ultimate units, the monads, “have no windows”: nothing comes in, nothing goes out. A grain that counts as one state from outside is a monad without windows.

Pascal, whose fear of infinite spaces I quoted last time, wrote in the same Pensées that we stand between two infinities, the infinitely large and the infinitely small, unable to see either end. He took this as a limit of human sight. Perhaps it is more than that. Perhaps each end is hidden by construction, from wherever anyone stands.

The contradiction disappears. Each level has a floor, but only for whoever counts from outside. The chain can go on forever because each grain is almost invisible to the one around it. The black hole formula does not forbid infinite layers. It only forbids seeing them.

Where information begins

I ended the first essay with “from nothing to nothing,” and the hope that the middle still matters. I would now say it differently.

Not from nothing to nothing. Nothing from outside, everything from inside, at every level, with no last level.

That makes my earlier ending more literal. If a universe has only one state until something inside it can keep a record, then the middle is not just where meaning lives. It is where information starts to exist. Not because anyone is conscious, but because something is somewhere.

This brings the question back to the usual subject of this blog. A large language model has a strangely similar shape. Before you ask it anything, it holds an enormous space of possible answers, all weighted, none chosen. Seen from outside, it is a horizon of possibilities with nothing in particular in it. An answer appears only when a question arrives from a particular place. Asking is not reading out something already written down. It is the moment the possibilities narrow into one.

This is an analogy, not physics. I already took the first essay’s question to an image generator in Decided in the Bulk, Written on the Boundary. But the lesson may be the same at both scales. Information is not stored somewhere, waiting. It happens at the boundary between a system and whatever stands inside it, or in front of it.

I started by believing the universe must be simple. Maybe it is. From outside, it is as simple as anything can be: one state. The complexity is a view from inside. And we are inside.

What I am not claiming

  • The one-state result comes mostly from simplified models, often with gravity in two dimensions. Physicists find it fairly convincing, but it is not settled for our universe.
  • Linking it to the de Sitter results is my own reading. As far as I know, none of the papers below states it this way.
  • Whether a 1987-style bubble really becomes a closed universe in the sense of these calculations is an open question.
  • The idea that the nesting never ends is speculation, not a result.
  • Hegel, Leibniz, Pascal and the comparison with AI are echoes, not evidence. They show the idea is old, not that it is true.
