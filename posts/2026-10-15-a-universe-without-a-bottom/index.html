A Universe Without a Bottom

It must be simpler than this.

essay
physics
personal
An evening with a physics paper I could not follow, and the questions it left: surfaces that know everything, grains within grains, and open ends.
Author

Stéphane Cuenat

Published

October 15, 2026

Something different, just this once. This blog is usually about AI, machines and measurement. This essay is not. It is a personal detour into physics, written by someone who is neither a physicist nor a mathematician, following a question out of curiosity. Its follow-up, Decided in the Bulk, Written on the Boundary, brings the question back to AI.

It must be simpler than this

I am not a physicist. I am not a mathematician. But one evening I opened a new physics paper, Holograms and Standard Models by Miyashita, Sekino and Susskind, and tried to follow it.

I got lost quickly. Fermions, ensembles, double-scaled limits, Feynman diagrams that look like flowers. And my first reaction was simple: something is wrong here. It must be easier. I believe the universe is fundamentally simple.

This essay is what happened when I followed that feeling. I didn’t find the answer. I found better questions, and maybe that is the point.

The surface knows everything

The first surprise came from black holes. In the 1970s, Bekenstein and Hawking found that the information a black hole can hold depends on the area of its surface, not on its volume.

S = \frac{A}{4\,\ell_P^2}

In plain words: cover the horizon with tiny tiles, each the size of the smallest length physics knows, and you have counted everything inside. Double the size of the black hole and its information grows four times, not eight.

This is strange. In everyday life a bigger box holds more things because it has more room inside. Here the inside seems to be written on the walls. Physicists call this the holographic principle: our three-dimensional world may be like an image projected from a flat surface.

So I asked myself: what if area and volume are not two different things? What if they are two faces of the same idea? It turns out physicists ask this too. Some say the area measures how much things are connected (entanglement) and the volume measures how complicated they are. Maybe space is just the shape that information takes when we look at it from inside.

Everything from nothing

Einstein did not change physics with more complicated equations. He took one simple idea completely seriously: light moves at the same speed for everyone. So I wondered if we are looking in the wrong place. What if there is nothing at all, and that nothing is creating everything?

To my surprise, the paper I could not follow says something close to this. The authors put their model at infinite temperature. That sounds extreme, but it means something simple: every possibility is equally likely. No preference, no structure, no information. It is the state of maximum ignorance, the closest thing to “nothing” that quantum physics allows.

And from this featureless state they claim a whole small world appears: particles, forces, even the tiny energy of empty space. Their motto is “set it and forget it.” You choose one number, how big the system is, and the rest follows.

Others had similar dreams. Some physicists noticed that the energy of matter and the energy of gravity may cancel exactly, so a universe might cost nothing to make. John Wheeler said “it from bit”: reality built from yes-or-no answers, information before matter. Of course, their “nothing” still has laws of physics in it. Why there are laws at all is still a mystery.

Grains within grains

Then another thought. What if we never reach the final piece? What if there is no end?

History points that way. We thought atoms were the bottom. Then we found the nucleus, then quarks. Each time the “fundamental” level turned out to be made of something else.

But the black hole formula seems to say no. If a region can hold only a limited amount of information, it cannot hide infinite layers inside. There must be a floor.

Unless the floor belongs only to us. What if what we call the universe is just one grain? The limit would apply inside our horizon, the part we can see, and not to everything. Some cosmologists already imagine this: space inflating forever, making bubble universes like ours, each one a grain, each with its own rules.

And it should work both ways. In 1987 physicists showed that a tiny bubble of very dense energy could grow into a new universe. From outside it would look like a small black hole that disappears. From inside it would be a whole cosmos, expanding, maybe one day full of people asking the same questions. A grain inside our grain. And maybe ours is a grain inside someone else’s.

The vertigo

At this point I felt something I did not expect. A kind of dizziness. Nothing creating everything. Grains inside grains. No final layer. Are we at the end of thinking, or just getting close to going a bit mad?

I don’t think it is madness. Pascal, more than three hundred years ago, wrote that the eternal silence of infinite spaces frightened him. Einstein said the most incomprehensible thing about the universe is that it is comprehensible. The dizziness seems to be what you feel when you follow the questions honestly.

The hard part is not the physics. It is accepting that we may go from nothing to nothing, with no final answer waiting for us. We want an end. We tell stories with endings. We look for the cause behind the cause. Being told the chain may never close feels like losing the ground under your feet.

The meaning is in the middle

Mathematics went through this once. In 1931 Kurt Gödel proved that mathematics can never be complete: there will always be true things it cannot prove. Many thought it was the end. It was not. Mathematics kept growing, and the open horizon became part of its beauty.

Maybe physics is the same. An end out of reach does not make the answers along the way less real. Feynman said he could live with doubt and not knowing, and that it was more interesting than having answers that might be wrong.

And “from nothing to nothing” forgets the middle. The middle is everything: stars, black holes, and someone on a quiet evening trying to understand a paper too difficult for them. If there is no final end, then asking is not a failure to arrive. Maybe it is what the universe does when it becomes complex enough to look at itself.

I started by believing the universe must be simple. I still believe it. But maybe simple does not mean finished.
