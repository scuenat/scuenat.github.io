What Survives Comparison

curiosity
physics
personal
Two observers inside one closed universe, each with their own records. Do they end up agreeing on one world? A third evening of curiosity, prompted by a reader.
Author

Stéphane Cuenat

Published

October 27, 2026

A reader’s question

After Nothing From Outside, Everything From Inside, a reader sent me a question I could not stop thinking about:

If two observers inside the same closed universe each keep their own records, do they always end up agreeing on one world?

My first answer was one word: no. That was true, but too short. “No” sounds like the two observers live in different worlds and that is the end of it. What I actually believe fits in one sentence: agreement is always agreement for someone. It exists for whoever holds both records and can compare them. It does not exist anywhere else.

In the last essay one observer came in to rescue the universe from having only one state. This essay puts in two, and follows that sentence through three cases. I am still not a physicist. This is still curiosity.

Nobody holds the master copy

The ordinary picture goes like this. There is one world, out there. Each observer takes notes on it. If two observers disagree, at least one made a mistake, and you can find out who by checking against the world.

In a closed universe that last step has nowhere to happen. There is no outside, so there is no referee holding the master copy. The previous essay said that the view from nowhere returns one state, which is to say nothing. So the world cannot be what guarantees agreement. Any agreement has to be produced from inside, by the observers themselves.

That is the real reason for my “no”. Not that observers must disagree, but that nothing is there to make them agree in advance.

The physicists who brought the observer back saw this coming. Harlow, Usatyuk and Zhao also consider two observers: each can treat the other as just more matter, and the rules require the two accounts to fit together. Agreement is not handed out by the universe. It is a condition the rules have to be built to satisfy, and the rules themselves are not settled yet.

Two observers, two pictures

The first way two records can differ came from the last essay. A bigger observer, one that can hold more, sees a richer universe. A smaller one sees a blurrier one.

Lay one record over the other, pixel by pixel, and they disagree almost everywhere. But that is the wrong comparison. The small observer simply did not record the fine detail. On what it did record, the large-scale shapes, the two agree.

So “do they agree?” only has a meaning on the questions both of them can ask. A blurry record and a sharp record do not contradict each other. One is just shorter.

Recent work suggests the differences can go further than blur. In 2024, De Vuyst, Eccles, Höhn and Kirklin showed that in quantum gravity even the entropy of a region, how much it can hide, depends on which observer describes it. Some quantities that looked like properties of the region turn out to belong to the region and the observer together.

When records meet

The second case is where quantum mechanics comes in.

Eugene Wigner imagined it in 1961. A friend sits in a sealed laboratory and measures an electron: up or down. She writes down the result. Wigner stands outside. As long as nothing leaks out, quantum mechanics tells Wigner to describe the whole lab, friend and notebook included, as still in a superposition of “saw up” and “saw down.” The friend has one result. Wigner has a description with both. Two observers, two accounts, and for now neither is wrong.

Since 2018 this has become sharper. Extended versions with two friends and two Wigners show that if every observer treats the others’ conclusions as facts, they reach a contradiction. In 2020 a team led by Kok-Wei Bong tested one such version, called Local Friendliness, in a real experiment. Its conclusion: you cannot keep all of these at once, that observed events are facts for everyone, that no influence travels faster than light, and that experimenters choose their settings freely. The “friends” in that experiment were simple photonic systems, so in 2023 Wiseman, Cavalcanti and Rieffel proposed a version where the friend can reason: an artificial intelligence running on a large quantum computer. Nobody can build it yet.

So quantum mechanics does not promise that one observer’s record is a fact for every other observer. That is the “no” again.

But look at what happens when Wigner opens the door and asks his friend what she saw. Now the two records interact, and quantum mechanics is firm about this case. Measure an electron, measure it again right away: same answer. Ask the friend, and her answer matches her notebook. There is one condition, and it is the heart of the paradoxes: while the lab is sealed, Wigner could in principle undo the friend’s observation, record included. The records whose truth is in doubt are the ones that never meet anything else, or are erased before they can. Records that are kept and brought together agree.

Rovelli’s relational quantum mechanics, which I mentioned last time, makes this a principle. In 2023 Adlam and Rovelli added a rule they call cross-perspective links: when one observer checks another’s record, what they find matches what the other recorded. Consistency is not a property of the world seen from above. It is a rule about meetings. The rule is debated, and so is relational quantum mechanics itself. But whichever view wins, agreement has to come from something that happens between observers.

That is what I meant in my reply. Two observers can tell different stories while apart. When they meet and compare, and their records have survived until then, quantum mechanics makes the stories fit. The agreement exists for them, at the meeting.

Why so many people agree so easily

There is an obvious objection. You and I can both look at the Moon from different continents, never talk, and still agree on where it is. Why does agreement feel automatic?

Wojciech Zurek’s answer is called quantum Darwinism. The Moon is hit by sunlight, and each photon that bounces off carries away a tiny copy of where it is. There are astronomical numbers of these copies: Riedel and Zurek estimated that a speck of dust one micrometre across, in sunlight for one microsecond, has its position copied roughly a hundred million times. You catch some copies; I catch others. We never touch the Moon or each other, but we have read different copies of the same record.

The striking part is how few copies you need. In the toy below, one bit is copied, imperfectly, into sixty small pieces of environment. Read a few percent and you already know the whole bit. Read more and you learn nothing new, until you hold almost all of it. That flat stretch, the plateau, is why the world looks objective: many observers, each with a small and different fraction, land on the same answer.

Zurek chose the word Darwinism on purpose. Only some properties survive being copied over and over without being disturbed, and those become shared, the same for everyone. The rest stay private, or are lost. Parts of this have since been proved in general and seen in the lab, with photons and with spins in diamond. It does not explain why there is one result rather than another, only why everyone sees the same one.

This is very close to the sentence I wrote in my reply before I knew the theory: reality is not what is out there, but what survives comparison. Zurek would say what survives copying. But copying is comparison done in advance, by the environment, on everyone’s behalf. It is still agreement for someone. There are just a great many someones.

Behind each other’s horizons

The third case is the strangest, and it is where my “no” becomes something else.

Our universe is expanding faster and faster, so it has a horizon, a little like a black hole turned inside out. If dark energy stays constant, light that a distant galaxy emits today will never reach us, however long we wait. In empty de Sitter space, the universe from the previous essay, every observer sits at the centre of their own horizon.

Take two observers beyond each other’s horizon, A and C. Neither can ever send the other a message. They can both see light from a shared distant past, so they may hold records of the same old events. But they can never bring those records together.

Do A and C agree on one world? For A and C themselves, the question has no answer. Not an unknown answer: no answer at all. Nothing either of them could ever do would settle it.

The diagram shows one subtlety. A third observer, D, between them, could catch an early message from each and compare them. But D could never tell A or C the result. So agreement between A and C may exist, but only for D. This is the opening sentence in its plainest form.

Physicists have met this before. In 1993 Susskind, Thorlacius and Uglum proposed black hole complementarity: someone who falls in and someone who stays out describe the horizon in incompatible ways, and both are right, because no one can ever hold both accounts. The idea was badly shaken by the firewall argument of 2012 and reshaped by the black-hole information results since 2019. In a form many physicists accept, its core survived: inside and outside are different encodings of the same thing, and no single observer can play them against each other.

In 2025 the same idea reached closed universes. Engelhardt, Gesteau and Harlow applied the new observer rules to a closed universe entangled with a pair of anti-de Sitter universes, the kind holography describes well, and called the result observer complementarity. An outside observer can run a test that says, in effect, there is no closed universe at all. An observer inside has their own consistent description of it. Here there is no D: nobody anywhere can receive messages from both. The result is contested: Antonini and collaborators (2025) and Higginbotham (2026) argued that the outside observer’s test cannot rule the closed universe out. But the debate is the reader’s question, now in the research literature.

So there is a kind of protection. Contradictions are allowed, but only where nobody can catch them.

One world, built

Put the three cases together.

  • Different sizes: their records differ, but on what both can see, they match.
  • When they meet: quantum mechanics makes their records consistent, and the environment often does the meeting for them.
  • Behind each other’s horizons: they can never compare, so for them “do they agree?” has no answer. At most a third observer holds it, and keeps it.

Nowhere in this list does the universe hand out one shared world from the start. One world is not given. It is what records build when they meet. It reaches exactly as far as meetings can reach, and it exists for whoever is at the meeting.

This also fits the grains from the last essay. A grain that pinches off into its own closed universe counts as one state from outside, and is everything to those inside. Now I see why the two descriptions never clash: once the grain has pinched off, nobody inside and nobody outside can ever compare notes, and there is no third place from which anyone could. The horizon that hides the grain also keeps both stories true. I did not know, when I wrote the last essay, that Engelhardt, Gesteau and Harlow had found something very close to this. I find that encouraging, and also a reason to be careful: their result is barely a year old and already disputed.

A short echo in the usual subject

This blog is usually about AI, so one short echo, as an analogy only. Ask two AI models the same question and they may answer differently. There is no master copy of the right answer inside either. What I trust is what survives comparison: answers checked against each other, against a test, against a shared source. Agreement between machines, like agreement between observers, is not stored somewhere. It is built, for someone, where records meet.

The echo also runs the other way. The most demanding Wigner’s friend experiment proposed so far needs a friend that can reason, and the candidate is an AI on a quantum computer. One day “do two observers agree?” may be tested with a machine in the friend’s seat.

What I am not claiming

  • Wigner’s friend, the extended versions (Frauchiger and Renner, 2018) and the Local Friendliness results are about quantum mechanics without gravity. Applying them to observers in a closed universe is my own step.
  • The extended Wigner’s friend results are still argued over. Different interpretations escape the contradiction in different ways; I have described the relational way because it fits these essays, not because it is settled. Relational quantum mechanics itself is under serious criticism.
  • The cross-perspective links rule is a postulate added to relational quantum mechanics, not something derived from it. Lawrence, Markiewicz and Żukowski argue that relative facts are incompatible with quantum theory; Cavalcanti, Di Biagio and Rovelli replied, and the exchange continued in 2025.
  • The rules for including observers in quantum gravity are recent and not unique: Abdalla, Antonini, Iliesiu and Levine proposed different rules, and the two proposals disagree on the number of states. Most of these results use simplified models, often gravity in two dimensions.
  • Black hole complementarity in its 1993 form did not survive the firewall debate unchanged. Among the replies, Harlow and Hayden argued that catching the contradiction would need a computation longer than the black hole’s life; from 2019, Penington and Almheiri, Engelhardt, Marolf and Maxfield showed, mostly in simplified models, how information leaves an evaporating black hole. I use complementarity as an example of the idea, not as established physics. Observer complementarity for closed universes is a 2025 proposal and is contested.
  • Whether our own universe has a permanent horizon depends on how dark energy behaves in the far future. Since 2024 the DESI survey has found hints that it may be weakening; if it fades enough, the horizon could eventually stop being one. The hints are not conclusive.
  • Quantum Darwinism explains why observers agree on an outcome, not why there is one outcome. Its general result (Brandão, Piani and Horodecki, 2015) is about which question observers can ask; agreement on the answer needs the redundancy, which depends on the model. The toy in Figure 2 is a textbook model with perfect symmetry; real environments are messier, though the plateau has been found in more realistic models and in experiments.
  • The connection to the grains is speculation built on speculation.
