The Disposable Screen

An AI agent that works through the backend needs almost no screen. When a person must see or decide something, it can draw the one screen that moment needs, then throw it away.

Essay, November 2026. A short and deliberately provocative essay about the screens we build for software. What happens to them once an AI agent can do the work behind them? It is a bet, not a forecast with numbers. Where I state a fact, I give the source; where I guess, I say so. The three figures are drawings of my own, and the code that draws them is linked under each one.

In short. We spend months designing the best screens for our software, and each person then uses a small part of them. I think most of that effort is about to lose its reason. A screen exists so that a person can reach what sits behind it: the backend, the functions that do the work and the data they work on. An AI agent can now call those functions directly. For most tasks, it needs no screen at all. When a person must see something or decide, the agent can draw a screen for that moment. It shows the facts and the one button this decision needs, and then it is thrown away. So the screen stops being something we define in advance, the same for everyone. The objections are serious: habit, trust, the weakness of typing, and screens that are made for looking. I think they move the border, but they do not stop the change. What survives is the backend, well described, plus the parts a screen is made of and the rules about what must always be shown.

1. The best screen nobody needed

Today, building software is, for a large part, building screens. A team agrees on what users need. A designer draws the pages. People test them and argue about them. The winning layout is coded page by page, then maintained for years.

I don’t want to laugh at this. A good screen is a real achievement. But watch what happens after it ships. One person opens it to change an address. Another approves one invoice. A third exports a report once a quarter. Each one touches a few fields and one button. The rest of the screen is there for someone else.

So the screen is a compromise. It is one drawing, defined in advance, that must serve everyone for every task. We call it the best interface, and for the average user it often is. For any one person on any one day, most of it is not needed.

2. The screen was the way in

Here is the point I most want to make. A screen is not the software. It is a way in. Behind every button sits a function in the backend: find this invoice, check that supplier, send this payment. The screen exists because, until now, a person had to drive those functions by hand, one click at a time, and needed something to click on.

Each wave of computing changed that way in. With punch cards, people wrote their wish in the machine’s terms and waited. With the command line, they typed one command at a time. With graphical screens, the machine showed its options and people pointed at them. Jakob Nielsen, who has studied usability for decades, argued in 2023 that AI is the first new way of using computers in sixty years (Nielsen 2023). In plain words: you say what you want, and the machine works out how.

What changed since then is that the how no longer needs a person at the controls. In November 2024, Anthropic published the Model Context Protocol, an open standard that lets an AI agent find and call the functions of other systems (Anthropic 2024b). A service that offers its functions this way describes them as data: what each one does, what it needs and what it returns. I wrote about that in The Interface Is Data. The agent reads the description and calls the functions itself. No menu, no page, no form.

Satya Nadella, the head of Microsoft, said something close at the end of 2024. He described most business applications as, in essence, databases with business rules on top, and said that those rules are moving to agents (Windows Central 2024). If he is right, what remains of an application is its backend. The pages in front of it were built for people, and people will need them less and less.

There is an irony here. In October 2024, Anthropic also taught its model to use a computer the way a person does: look at the screen, move the cursor, click and type (Anthropic 2024a). That is useful when a system offers no other way in. But it shows how strong our habit is. We taught a machine to use screens that were made for humans. When the backend is open, the agent does not need them.

A person still needs a screen sometimes: to see a result, to choose between options, to confirm a payment. And here comes the second change. The agent can draw that screen itself, for that moment. In 2025, researchers at Google showed it. Given good instructions and tools, a large language model can build a custom, interactive interface for almost any request (Google Research 2025). In their tests, people preferred these generated screens to the usual text answer more than eight times out of ten. Screens made by human experts were still better, but the generated ones matched them in about half of the cases. The researchers say plainly that their tests ignored speed, and that drawing a screen could take a minute or two (Leviathan et al. 2026). In the same year, OpenAI let services show small interactive apps inside its chat assistant (OpenAI 2025). And the Model Context Protocol gained a standard way for a tool to send an interface with its answer (Model Context Protocol 2025).

Put the two changes together. For most tasks, there is no screen: the agent works through the backend. When a person is needed, the screen is drawn for that moment, used once and thrown away. That is my claim. The screen becomes disposable. And the generic screen, the one we design in advance for everyone, is no longer the one we need to define.

3. What could disappear

Let me be provocative and list what I think could go, not in a year, but over ten years or so.

  • Navigation. Menus, tabs, sidebars, the search box that finds the right page. They exist because the screen is big and you must find your corner of it. When the agent finds the function for you, there is no corner to find.
  • The app as a place. Today you go to an app: you open it, log in and look for the page. Tomorrow the app may come to you, as a small screen inside whatever you are already doing, and leave when you are done.
  • The general form. The form with forty fields, of which you fill four. The agent already knows the other thirty-six, or knows that they don’t matter for you.
  • Automation that clicks. A large part of business automation today is robots that click on screens made for people. The industry calls it robotic process automation. These robots copy a value from one window into another, because the two systems have no other way to talk. Move a button and the robot breaks. An agent that calls the backend has nothing to break.
  • One page per feature. Much of a software team’s work is building a new page for each new feature. If the agent calls the function, and draws a screen only when one is needed, the team builds the function, describes it well, and stops there.

None of this means that software disappears. The backend stays, and it matters more than ever. In Intent Is the Source I argued that for programmers, intent and checks become the source, and code becomes an output. This is the same move, one floor up. For the people who use software, intent becomes the input, and the screen becomes an output, when it is needed at all.

4. Four good reasons I could be wrong

Now I must be fair. I expect four objections from people who build software. Each one is strong. I will give each its full weight, then say how far I think it can be pushed back.

People cannot learn a screen that keeps changing

This objection has data behind it. In 2004, Leah Findlater and Joanna McGrenere compared three kinds of menus (Findlater and McGrenere 2004). One never changed. One moved your most used items to the top by itself. One let you arrange it yourself. The menu that never changed was faster than the one that changed by itself, and most people preferred the one they arranged themselves. Around the same years, some office software hid the menu items you had not used recently. That idea did not survive the next redesign. People want to know where things are.

My answer: habit matters most when you do the same task again and again. For those tasks, the agent should stop changing the screen. It can draw it once, let the person adjust it, and then keep it. Notice, too, what most of our habits are for: finding our way through the navigation. If the agent finds the function, there is less to remember. And the winner of that study, the menu people arranged themselves, points the same way. People did not want a screen that changes behind their back. They wanted one shaped to them, under their control. An agent that asks “keep it like this?” can give them that.

You cannot test a screen that does not exist yet

A bank, a hospital or a regulator needs the same flow every time. They test it, certify it, train people on it, and use it as evidence when something goes wrong. How do you test a million screens that each existed once?

This is the hardest of the four, and I think regulated flows will keep fixed screens the longest. But I believe the answer is to test the rules, not the pictures. The screen is drawn from a fixed set of tested parts, and it follows fixed rules. Before any payment, show the amount, the recipient and the date, and ask for a clear confirmation. A program, not the agent, can check those rules on every screen before it is shown. I proposed something close for tools in A Grammar for Tools: rules set by policy, which no agent can skip. And the exact screen a person saw can be recorded. As evidence, that is better than “it was version 4.2 of the form”.

Fixed screens are also less safe than we like to think. People skip warnings they have seen a hundred times, and click the wrong row in a long table. A screen that shows only what matters for this one decision might lead to fewer mistakes. That is a hunch, and it should be measured.

Typing is a step back

We left the command line for graphical screens for good reasons. On a screen you see your options; at a prompt you must remember them. Nielsen himself names the problem: many people find it hard to put what they want into words (Nielsen 2023). In 1997, Ben Shneiderman and Pattie Maes held a famous debate on this question (Shneiderman and Maes 1997). He defended direct manipulation: people should see what they act on, and stay in control. She argued that some of the work must be handed to software agents. Andrej Karpathy, who helped build the AI teams at Tesla and OpenAI, made a related point in 2025. Graphical interfaces still matter, he said, because they let a person check an AI’s work quickly (Karpathy 2025).

I agree with him, and this is not chat. Chat, voice, or a click on something you are already looking at, is only how the request starts. What comes back, when a person is needed, is a real screen, with buttons and sliders that you see and control. That is direct manipulation, as Shneiderman wanted, drawn by an agent, as Maes wanted. It is also the screen Karpathy asks for: one made for checking this result, and nothing else. In a way, the debate of 1997 ends with both sides right.

Many screens are for looking, not doing

Not every screen is a task to finish. A map, a drawing canvas, a code editor, a dashboard: we use them to look around, to explore and to think. An agent that hands you three fields and a button has misunderstood why you came.

Here I partly agree. Instruments stay. A violin is not rebuilt for each song. The tools of a craft, the ones we shape by hand over years, will stay fixed because we want them fixed. But even here, look at dashboards. I wrote two earlier posts about them, Show What Changed, Not the Data and What a Quiet Screen Cannot See. A dashboard, I argued, should show only what changed in our understanding, so that most weeks it is almost empty. That is a disposable screen too: different every week, drawn for what happened.

So I don’t think the four objections save the fixed screen everywhere. They draw its new borders: daily tasks, decisions that must look the same every time, and instruments. Everywhere else, the agent works through the backend, and the screen, when there is one, is drawn for the moment. I believe that covers most of the software we use at work, but I have not measured it.

5. What stays, and who builds it

If the screen is disposable, what is left to build? Quite a lot, but in different places.

First, the backend. Its functions become the real product. They must be described well enough for an agent to use them safely: what each one does, what it needs, what it changes, and whether it is safe to retry. This is where much of a software team’s time will go.

Second, the parts and the rules. The parts are the buttons, fields, tables and charts. Each one is built and tested once, and made usable for everyone, including people who cannot see the screen well. The rules say how the parts may be combined. Above all, they say what must always be shown: the amount before a payment, the dose before a prescription, the warning before a deletion. Google’s A2UI project, opened in December 2025, works exactly this way (Google for Developers 2025). The agent sends a description of the interface as data, and the app draws it only from a catalogue of parts it trusts. The agent chooses; the parts and the rules stay fixed.

So designers do not disappear. Kate Moran and Sarah Gibbons, of the Nielsen Norman Group, call this outcome-oriented design (Moran and Gibbons 2024). Designers set the goals and the limits, and the system composes the screen within them. It is a little like printing. Almost nobody sets type by hand anymore, but someone still designs every typeface we read, and the rules of a good page.

There is a chance here that I find moving. A screen drawn for one person can be drawn for that person’s eyes, hands and language: large text, fewer choices, plain words. A fixed screen offers these only as settings, often added late. A disposable screen could start from them. That is a hope, not a fact. It will only happen if the rules ask for it.

6. How I would know I was wrong

An essay like this should say what would prove it wrong. Forecasts point both ways. In 2024, the research firm Gartner predicted that by 2028 a third of enterprise software would include AI agents, up from less than one percent. In 2025, the same firm predicted that more than forty percent of agentic AI projects would be cancelled by the end of 2027 (Gartner 2025). Neither proves anything. So here is my own test.

Look at the software people use at work five years from now. Suppose most tasks still start by opening an app, finding a menu and filling in a fixed form. Then I was wrong. Suppose people who tried disposable screens went back to fixed ones, the way they turned away from menus that moved by themselves. Then I was wrong too. And if regulators refuse rule-checked screens even for simple, low-risk tasks, I was at least too early.

Now suppose the opposite. The app quietly stops being a place you go, most work happens through the backend, and the few screens you see are small and fitted to the moment. Then the change has happened, probably without much noise. Mark Weiser was a computer scientist at Xerox’s research centre in Palo Alto. In 1991, he opened a famous article with this sentence: “The most profound technologies are those that disappear” (Weiser 1991). He meant computers themselves. I think the screen may be next.

Here is a question for anyone about to design a new screen. Could an agent do this task through the backend, and draw what the person must see at the moment they need it? If yes, spend the effort on the backend, the parts and the rules. Let the screen follow.

7. A map on a napkin

Anyone who has asked for directions in a café knows the napkin map. The person behind the counter takes a pen and draws three streets, a church and an arrow. It is not pretty, and it is not to scale. It shows only your way, and it is exactly what you need. When you arrive, you throw it away.

For decades we have printed better and better city maps, the same for everyone, and asked each person to find their own way on them. I think the next interface looks more like the napkin. Most of the time, someone simply takes you there. When you need to see the way, it is drawn for you, right now, and thrown away when you arrive.
