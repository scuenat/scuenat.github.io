Two Cores and an Idea

A pencil wrote the pages. A data centre read them. Which one made the discovery?

essay

AI just helped read Gödel’s lost notebooks. Do we need huge machines to discover things? My small experiment on two processor cores says: not to start.
Author

Stéphane Cuenat

Published

November 17, 2026

Draft v7, not yet published. Revised November 17, 2026. This piece is under review. It is not listed anywhere else on the site and may still change. All drafts.

Essay, November 2026. A short, personal essay. It starts from a news story about Kurt Gödel’s notebooks, read with the help of AI. And it starts from a small experiment of my own, run on two ordinary processor cores. It asks a plain question: to discover something, do we really need huge machines? Two figures are schematics of my own. One draws the Gödel numbers as reported in the press. Two show the numbers of my own experiment, and the last of these is preliminary. The code that draws them all is linked under each one.

In short. In the 1940s, one man with a pencil filled a thousand pages. This year, a machine helped read them. The pencil did the discovering; the machine did the reading. That contrast stayed with me, because the same week I had tested an idea of my own on two processor cores. The small test already showed something surprising. Big machines matter: to check a result, and to read what no person has time to read. But they come second. A small machine is enough to start. An empty mind is not.

1. A pencil wrote it, a machine read it

A few days ago I came across a post on LinkedIn. It said that an AI, Anthropic’s Claude, had helped decode Kurt Gödel’s old notebooks. Hundreds of theorems, hidden for almost a century.

It caught my eye for a personal reason. That same week, I had been testing an idea of my own, on a very small computer. So the question came at once: what did the machine really do here, and what did the people do?

I wanted the real story, so I looked it up. The news was reported by the Guardian on 13 November 2026 (The Guardian 2026), and other outlets agree on the main facts (AI TL;DR 2026; Archyde 2026; MLQ.ai 2026). In the early 1940s, Gödel filled sixteen notebooks, about 1,009 pages, with mathematics and logic. He wrote much of it in Gabelsberger, an old German shorthand that almost nobody reads any more. Since 2017, the logician Jan von Plato, in Helsinki, has studied them. This is not new work for him: he had already published a whole book on Gödel’s shorthand notes from 1930 (Plato 2020). By hand, the reading went at about a page a week.

The detail I like most is this one. The AI did not read the shorthand. Von Plato did. He taught himself the shorthand, transcribed the pages, and wrote six books about Gödel. Then he gave all of it to the AI. It came back with a long explanation of the notebooks and a list of 663 results that Gödel had proved, sketched or outlined. Checked with a proof assistant, a program that verifies every step of a proof, about 400 held as stated. About 200 needed a repair, and about 50 were wrong. Gödel had sometimes marked his own notes as wrong, so that last part is not a surprise.

One result stands out. As reported, Gödel came very close to something Paul Cohen proved in 1963. It is about one of the basic rules of set theory, the axiom of choice: it can be neither proved nor refuted from the others. Gödel claimed later in life that he had found it in the 1940s. The notebooks seem to show he was one definition away. One detail in the post was wrong, though: Gödel did not prove the continuum hypothesis. He showed that it cannot be disproved (Gödel 1940), and Cohen later showed that it cannot be proved either (Cohen 1963).

So look at the order. Years of a mind to write the pages. Years of another mind to learn to read them. Then a machine, to read them all at once. The machine came last, and it came fast. And it is not finished. The logician Joel David Hamkins noted that historians still have to sort out how much is Gödel and how much is the machine’s reconstruction (The Guardian 2026; AI TL;DR 2026).

2. Almost no machine, a lot of mind

Now, look at what Gödel had. Paper, a pencil, and a way of writing so private that it took a man years to learn it again.

With that, in 1931, he showed that mathematics can never be complete: in any honest system strong enough to count, there are true statements it cannot prove (Gödel 1931). Five years later, Alan Turing used the same kind of thinking to describe a machine that can compute anything computable. And he showed that some problems no machine can ever solve (Turing 1937). The whole theory of the computer you are reading this on came out of that.

No data centre. No graphics cards. Not even a computer, because the computer did not exist yet. They were the ones who imagined it. The machine was not their tool. It was their idea.

Today we talk as if discovery needs a huge machine and the power of a small town. Decades ago, minds found almost everything we now build on, with almost nothing. AI will surely take us to a next level, and we will use it. But do we absolutely need it? I wanted to test that question on myself.

3. My own small test

Machine learning is not new to me. My PhD used deep learning for holography, I still write research papers with a research lab in Besançon, and at work I bring AI into software. But I don’t spend my days designing new kinds of networks. This idea came on the side, and it would not leave me.

When a language model reads a text, it uses a step called attention. Each word looks at the other words and pulls in what it needs. I kept seeing that step as a landscape. Each fact in the text digs a well. The question is a ball. One step of attention is the ball moving once towards the right well. This picture is not only mine: researchers showed in 2020 that one attention step is exactly one step of a kind of memory network built on wells (Ramsauer et al. 2021).

So I asked a simple question. Why stop after one step? Let the ball roll until it rests. And let the text itself shape the landscape, new for every text.

To test it, I used a simple task. The text is a list of pairs of random symbols: A points to B, B points to C, and so on. The question gives a start. The network must follow the chain to its end. Each link is one hop. The symbols change every time, so the network cannot memorise the answers. It has to learn how to follow.

Now I must be honest about how this was built. The idea, the landscape and the ball, is mine. But an AI assistant helped me write the code, run the tests and write the research plan. Without it, this would have taken me months of evenings, not a few days. So I did use AI. What I did not use is a big machine. Everything ran on two ordinary processor cores. The networks are tiny: between 50,000 and 90,000 numbers to learn. The large language models people talk about have hundreds of billions. A single training run took a few minutes.

4. What the small machine found

The first result was a failure, and I learned more from it than from the successes.

The network found the right answer, then rolled past it. At the step it was trained for, the answer was there. If I let it roll sixteen times longer, it was right only one time in five. The answer was not a place where the ball rests. It was a place the ball passes by. Researchers call this overthinking, and it is well known in networks that repeat the same step (Bansal et al. 2022). The fix is also known: keep the question inside the landscape, not only at the start. With that change, the ball stayed with the answer, even after rolling sixteen times longer, and even when it started from a random place.

I like this failure, because it is the essay in small. An answer that only passes by is not knowledge. What made it stay was keeping the question present the whole time.

Then came the surprise. I trained the network on chains of one to three hops only. Then I gave it longer chains, which it had never seen. With four hops, it was right 94% of the time. With five hops, 77%. A standard attention network with three or five layers got none of them right.

Was it the rolling, or the way the wells are dug? I made a cleaner test. Same network, same code, one change. In one version, each well is dug on its own, independently of the others. In the other version, the wells compete, the way they do in normal attention: if one gets deeper, the others get shallower. Both versions learned the training chains equally well. On longer chains, they split.

With independent wells, the network was right 81% of the time on four hops and 58% on five. With competing wells, 32% and 3%. I think I understand why. When the wells compete, the ball always feels a mix of all of them. When they are independent, it can listen only to the well it is sitting in, and then move on to the next one.

I want to be careful here. This is two training runs, on a toy task, with tiny networks. It may not hold at a larger scale. But it is a real difference, found with almost nothing.

5. The objection: then is not now

There is a strong answer to everything I just wrote, and I want to give it its full weight.

It goes like this. Gödel and Turing worked on empty ground. The big, simple discoveries were still there to be found. Today, the easy ground is taken. And in AI itself, history has been harsh with clever ideas. The researcher Richard Sutton called it the bitter lesson: over seventy years, methods that simply used more computing power beat methods built on human insight, again and again (Sutton 2019). Language models did not come from a beautiful idea about wells. They came from scale. Seen that way, my two cores are nostalgia.

I think that is partly right. But it mixes up two moments: when an idea is born, and when it is proven to win.

The bitter lesson is about who wins at the end, on the big benchmarks. It says little about where ideas come from. Attention itself began as an idea in a paper on translation, years before anyone spent millions on it (Bahdanau et al. 2015). Scale tells you which idea wins. It cannot tell you which idea to have.

The Gödel story, read closely, says the same. The hardest step was not done by the machine. A man spent years learning a dead shorthand, by hand, so the pages could be read at all. The machine then did what no single person has time to do: read everything, connect it, list it. It did in a short time what would have taken one person a very long time. That is a huge help. But it came second.

My own experiment shows the other side too, and I should say it. My small result could vanish. The next step in my plan is a test on a real graphics card, with five runs per model instead of two, and properly tuned rivals. If the gap closes, my idea was wrong, and I will need a bigger machine to know it. So big machines are not useless. They are just not first. I used an AI assistant too, and it saved me months. But it did not give me the picture of the ball in the landscape. That came the old way, in my head.

6. A small machine, not an empty mind

That last sentence needs a correction, because it can sound as if ideas fall from the sky. They don’t. Gödel’s pencil had years of study behind it. Von Plato’s reading took years of learning a dead shorthand. My ball in the landscape came from years of working with networks, first in my PhD, then at work. Without that, two cores would have tested nothing worth testing.

That is the real contrast, and the one I want to keep. It is not the pencil against the data centre. It is a small machine against an empty mind. The first is enough to start. The second is not, whatever machine you give it. What makes a discovery possible is not the size of the computer. It is the time a person has spent with a question.

The order follows from that. First the idea, from a mind that has prepared for it. Then a small test that could prove it wrong. Then, only if it survives, a big machine to check it and to scale it.

AI will take us further. I am sure of that, and I am glad of it. It reads what we have no time to read, and it builds what we have no time to build. But it still needs someone to bring it a question worth asking.

So the next time a problem seems to need a bigger machine, I will ask myself one thing first. Is my problem too big for my machine? Or is my question too small for my mind?

Gödel had a pencil. The machine that read him had a data centre. Only one of them was asking.
