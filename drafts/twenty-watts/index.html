Twenty Watts

A machine wrote 250,000 pages about the primes, and we still don’t know if it thinks. Why I believe the AI that will matter is small, close to us, and worth waiting for.

Essay, November 2026. A short essay about us and the machines we are building. It is more a reflection than an argument with data, and where I give a fact, I give the source. The figure is a drawing of my own, and the code that draws it is linked under it.

In short. A machine has reportedly written a proof of the most famous problem in mathematics. Written out, it would run past 250,000 pages, and nobody can read it. I don’t know if such a machine is intelligent. Maybe it only tries possibilities so fast that it looks intelligent to us. Our brains search too, but they search very little, and they run on about twenty watts, the power of a small light bulb. That is the point I keep coming back to. We are the living proof that a mind does not need a power station. So I believe the AI that will matter for us is not the giant in the data centre. It is a small brain, close to us, good at many things. It will take time to build. I think we should take that time, instead of running.

1. A book nobody will read

Three days ago, a mathematician at Anthropic announced that he and Claude, the company’s AI, had proved the Riemann hypothesis. It is a question about the prime numbers, open for 167 years. As reported, a computer has checked every step. In a footnote, he says that the proof, written out in prose, ran past 250,000 pages. He chose not to post it (Scientific American 2026; OfficeChai 2026).

I wrote about that day already, in Long First, Short Later. There, I bet that people will find the short proof hidden inside the long one. Today I want to ask a simpler question, and a harder one.

Does it help us?

Think of 250,000 pages. If you read one page every minute, eight hours a day, it would take you about a year and a half. And you would not understand it at the end, because a proof that long was never written for a person. So here we have a truth, perhaps the most wanted truth in mathematics, sealed inside a book that no person will ever read.

Maybe it helps. If the proof holds, hundreds of results that begin with “assuming the Riemann hypothesis” become certain. That is real. But maybe it does not help at all. Maybe it is like being told how a story ends, with no way to read the story. We know that it ends well. We don’t know how, and we don’t know why.

I honestly don’t know which one it is. And I think that not knowing is where the real question starts.

2. Does it think?

We built a machine that we do not understand.

I don’t say this to be dramatic. The people who build these systems say it too. In 2025, Dario Amodei, the head of Anthropic, wrote that people are often “surprised and alarmed” to learn this. In his words, “we do not understand how our own AI creations work” (Amodei 2025). We know how to train them. We can measure what they do. But when one of them finds a proof, nobody can point inside and say: here is where it understood.

So is it intelligent? I go back and forth. Some days I watch it solve a problem I could not solve, and I think: yes, obviously. Other days I think it is something else, something we have no word for yet.

Alan Turing faced the same question in 1950. He opened his famous paper with it: “Can machines think?” Then he put it aside. He wrote that he believed the question was “too meaningless to deserve discussion”, and he replaced it with a test of behaviour instead (Turing 1950). Decades later, the computer scientist Edsger Dijkstra put it more sharply. He said that asking whether machines can think is about as relevant as asking whether submarines can swim (Dijkstra 1984).

I like Dijkstra’s line, but I don’t fully agree with it. For a submarine, it does not matter. For us, I think it does. If we call something intelligent, we start to trust it like we trust a mind. We let it decide. We stop checking.

Here is my doubt, said plainly. These machines can walk through an enormous number of possibilities, far faster than we ever could. When something walks that fast, it can look like thinking from the outside. A magician’s hands, if they are fast enough, look like magic. The machine may not be intelligent. It may only make us think it is intelligent. And I am not sure we can tell the difference from where we stand.

3. “But we search too”

Now, here is the strongest objection, and it is a good one.

It goes like this. You say the machine only searches. But what do you think your brain does? Your brain is a search machine too. Evolution shaped it over hundreds of millions of years, by trying and failing. Then you spent twenty years of childhood and school training it. When you “just see” the answer, that is millions of past searches, packed into a habit. If searching fast is not intelligence, then you are not intelligent either.

I feel the force of this. I cannot prove that my thinking is anything more than a search, and I don’t think anyone can. Maybe the line between “searching” and “understanding” is not a line at all.

But look at how much each one searches.

In 1997, a chess computer, Deep Blue, beat the world champion. Its makers describe a machine that examined well over a hundred million positions every second (Campbell et al. 2002). Long before that, in the 1940s, Adriaan de Groot ran an experiment. He was a Dutch psychologist, and a strong chess player himself. He asked good players to think aloud while choosing a move. The grandmasters did not look at more moves than weaker players. They looked at a handful. What made them strong was which moves they saw first (de Groot 1965).

So maybe that is the difference, and it is not the one I expected. Not search against no search. A lot of search against very little. Here is how I would say it in one sentence. A machine that can try everything does not need to understand anything; we could never try everything, so we had to understand.

If that is right, then understanding is not the opposite of searching. It is what you build when you cannot afford to search. It is a way of being frugal with possibilities.

4. Twenty watts

Now let me move from the mind to the body, because this is where the idea became concrete for me.

The human brain is about two percent of the body’s weight, but it uses about a fifth of its energy (Raichle and Gusnard 2002). In power, that is about twenty watts (Balasubramanian 2021). A small light bulb. With that, you learned a language from a few people around you, not from the whole internet. You can recognise a face after years, cross a busy street, and comfort a child. And, now and then, you have an idea that nobody had before.

Compare this with what we are building. The International Energy Agency estimates that data centres used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024. That is around one and a half percent of all the electricity in the world. It expects that to more than double by 2030, mostly because of AI. A typical data centre built for AI uses as much electricity as a hundred thousand homes. The largest ones now being built will use twenty times more (International Energy Agency 2025).

I am not saying these numbers measure the same thing. A data centre serves millions of people at once, and a brain serves one. But the gap is so large that it says something on its own.

We are the proof. A mind that learns, adapts, invents and cares can run on twenty watts. Nature found the way. It did not find it by being fast. It found it by taking a very long time.

5. The brain that will matter

I want to be clear: I am not against AI. I use it every day, and I have written here about how much it changed my own work.

But I believe the AI that will matter for us, in our lives, is not the giant model in a data centre that we rent by the word. It is a smaller brain, running close to us: on our computer, in our phone, in our house, in the machine on the factory floor. It will not know everything. But it will be really competent at many things. It will learn from us and stay with us, and it will work without asking permission from a server on another continent.

I wrote about what that could look like in An Ecosystem, Not an Oracle. Not one oracle that knows everything, but many small specialists, a shared memory and a loop that keeps learning. And in Fast AI, I argued that a system should learn slowly, once, and then act fast, every time. That is what a brain does.

Someone will say: the small models exist only because the big ones taught them. That may be true today. The big models may be the scaffolding, the thing you need while you build and take down after. But nobody mistakes the scaffolding for the house.

6. Take the time

So why are we running?

I understand some of the reasons. Competition. Money. Fear of being second. And the real excitement of watching something new happen every week. I feel that excitement too.

But look at what nature did. It took hundreds of millions of years to make a brain that runs on twenty watts. Then each of us took twenty years to fill one. Nothing about the best mind we know was fast. It was slow, cheap and patient. Maybe that is not a coincidence. Maybe a good mind is what you get when you cannot afford to be wasteful.

We may need time to get there with our machines too. Time to understand what we have built before we build it ten times bigger. Time to find out whether a 250,000-page proof can become a ten-page one that a student can hold. Time to make the small brain good, rather than the big one bigger.

I don’t think taking that time means falling behind. I think it may be the shortest road.

We already carry the proof of what is possible. It sits behind our eyes, it runs on twenty watts, and it took a very long time to make. We could take a little time too.
