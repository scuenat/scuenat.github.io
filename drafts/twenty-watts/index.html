Twenty Watts

Time made a mind that runs on twenty watts. We are trying to skip the time, we pay for it in data and power, and I am not sure we gain as much as we think.

Essay, November 2026. A short essay about us and the machines we are building. It is a reflection more than an argument with data; where I give a fact, I give the source. The figure is a drawing of my own, and the code that draws it is linked under it.

In short. A machine has reportedly proved the most famous problem in mathematics, in a proof that would run past 250,000 pages. Is it intelligent? It does more than search: it finds things nobody found before. But discovery, for it and for us, may be a search in the end. The real difference lies elsewhere. Nobody designed our brain. Time made it, over hundreds of millions of years, and then a childhood. What time made runs on about twenty watts, a small light bulb. Now we want the same mind without the time. But time does not vanish when we skip it. We pay for it in another currency: data, which is other people’s time stored, and power. And what do we gain? Sometimes a lot. But the answers get faster, and we do not. So I believe the AI that will matter is a small brain, close to us. And I think we should give it some of the time we are trying to skip.

1. A book nobody will read

Three days ago, a mathematician at Anthropic announced that he and Claude, the company’s AI, had proved the Riemann hypothesis. It is a question about the prime numbers, open for 167 years. As reported, a computer has checked every step. Written out in prose, the proof ran past 250,000 pages, and he chose not to post it (Scientific American 2026; OfficeChai 2026). I wrote about that day in Long First, Short Later.

Read one page a minute, eight hours a day, and you would need a year and a half. So here is a truth, perhaps the most wanted in mathematics, sealed in a book that no person will ever read.

Does it help us? Maybe. If it holds, hundreds of results that begin with “assuming the Riemann hypothesis” become certain. Or maybe not at all. It is like being told how a story ends, with no way to read the story. We know that. We don’t know why.

2. Searching, or discovering?

The easy answer is that the machine only searches. It walks through possibilities far faster than we ever could. And speed looks like thinking from the outside, the way a magician’s fast hands look like magic.

I don’t think that answer is honest anymore. These systems now find things nobody had found: a counterexample to an old conjecture, a formula in physics, a proof nobody wrote (Partly Wrong, Mostly Right). That looks like discovery. Sometimes it looks like invention.

So here is the harder question. What is a discovery, if not a search that ends somewhere new? A discovery finds what was already there. An invention makes what was not. But both may begin the same way: try, fail, keep what works. Our brains do that. Evolution did it for hundreds of millions of years, before there was anyone to call it intelligent.

If so, there may be no line between searching and inventing. Only a difference in how much searching it takes.

In 1997, the chess computer Deep Blue beat the world champion. Its makers describe a machine that examined well over a hundred million positions every second (Campbell et al. 2002). Decades earlier, the Dutch psychologist Adriaan de Groot asked strong players to think aloud while choosing a move. The grandmasters did not look at more moves than weaker players. They looked at a handful. What made them strong was which moves they saw first (de Groot 1965).

Here it is in one sentence. A machine that can try everything does not need to understand anything. We could never try everything, so we had to understand.

3. Time made us

Do we need a giant brain to get there? I don’t think so, because we did not come from one.

Nobody designed the human brain. Time made it: hundreds of millions of years of small changes, most of them failures, kept only when they worked. Then each of us added about twenty years of childhood, school, mistakes, and a few people who taught us.

What time made is small. The brain is about two percent of the body’s weight and uses about a fifth of its energy (Raichle and Gusnard 2002). That is about twenty watts (Balasubramanian 2021), a small light bulb. With it, you learned a language from the people around you, not from the whole internet. You can recognise a face after years. And now and then, you have an idea nobody had before.

We are the proof that a mind does not need a power station. It needs time.

4. The price of skipping time

Time is exactly what we don’t want to spend. We want the mind now, in a few years, not in a few hundred million.

But here is what I think we forget. Time does not vanish when we skip it. We pay for it in another currency.

What evolution did slowly, in tiny steps and with almost no energy, we try to do in a few years. To make up for the missing time, we pour in data. One open model from 2024, Llama 3, was trained on more than fifteen trillion tokens, the small pieces of words that these models read (Meta 2024). By my own rough count, a person reading eight hours a day would need thousands of lifetimes to get through that.

Now notice what that data is. Every page of it was written by a person, over centuries. So we did not really skip the time. We borrowed it, from everyone who ever wrote. Data is time, stored.

And we pour in power. The International Energy Agency estimates that data centres used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024. That is around one and a half percent of all the electricity in the world. It expects this to more than double by 2030, mostly because of AI. A typical data centre built for AI uses as much electricity as a hundred thousand homes (International Energy Agency 2025).

So the trade is simple. Nature paid in time. We pay in stored time and power. The less time we give, the more data and power we need. Shrinking the time does not make the mind smaller. It makes it bigger.

5. What do we gain?

Now let me turn the question on myself. Suppose we could shrink that time to almost zero: a question in the morning, the answer by night. What do we really gain, as human beings? Is it as much as we believe?

Sometimes, yes. Innovation is not always bad, and speed is not always haste. When a disease spreads, every month saved is lives saved. A proof found in 41 days instead of twelve years frees those years for something else. I would not want to give that back.

But look at what does not get faster. A child still needs twenty years to grow. A person still needs time to understand a new idea, to trust it, and to decide what it means. A society still needs years to learn to live with a new tool. The answers arrive faster. We do not. So the time we save on finding, we often owe on understanding. The 250,000 pages are the example: the machine saved years, and gave us a book nobody can read.

Two thousand years ago, the Roman philosopher Seneca wrote that our life is not short; we waste much of it (Seneca 2004). I think of that line when I hear that we must go faster. Maybe the problem was never that we had too little time. Maybe it was what we do with the time we have.

6. A small brain, close to us

I am not against AI. I use it in my work every day. But I believe the AI that will matter in our lives is not the giant we rent by the word. It is a small brain, close to us: in our phone, our house, the machine on the factory floor. Good at many things, learning from us, staying with us. I sketched it in An Ecosystem, Not an Oracle and Fast AI.

The big models may be how we get there: the scaffolding you need while you build. But nobody mistakes the scaffolding for the house.

7. Give it time

So why are we running? Competition, money, the fear of being second. And the real excitement of something new every week. I feel it too.

But the best mind we know was not made fast. It was made slowly, cheaply and patiently. Maybe that is no coincidence. Maybe a good mind is what you get when you cannot afford to waste.

Time made a mind that runs on twenty watts. Haste is making minds that need a power station. We cannot skip the time for free. We can only choose how to pay for it.

I would rather pay in time.
