Borrowed Time

Time made a mind that learns on twenty watts. Our machines borrow that time from everything we ever wrote, and borrowed time is always paid back.

Essay, November 2026. A short essay about us and the machines we are building. It is a reflection more than an argument with data. Where I give a fact, I give the source, and where I borrow an idea from physics, I say how far the comparison goes. The first figure is a drawing of my own; the second sketches a known result from physics. The code that draws them is linked under each one.

In short. A machine has reportedly proved the Riemann hypothesis, in a proof that would run past 250,000 pages. Is it intelligent? Maybe all discovery, ours included, is a search in the end. What differs is how the search was paid for. Selection and then a childhood made our brain, slowly, and it learns all its life on about twenty watts. Our machines skip the wait. They borrow the time instead: from everything people ever wrote, and from power stations. Physics warns that past a certain speed, you get less, not more. I think that can happen to us too. Borrowed time is paid back, with interest: a proof nobody can read must still be understood. So I believe the AI that will matter is a small brain, close to us, and given time.

1. A book nobody will read

Three days ago, a mathematician at Anthropic announced that he and Claude, the company’s AI, had proved the Riemann hypothesis. It is a question about the prime numbers, open for 167 years. As reported, a computer has checked every step. Written out in prose, the proof ran past 250,000 pages, and he chose not to post it (Scientific American 2026; OfficeChai 2026). I wrote about that day in Long First, Short Later.

Read one page a minute, eight hours a day, and you would need a year and a half. So here is a truth, perhaps the most wanted in mathematics, sealed in a book that no person will ever read.

Does it help us? Maybe. If it holds, hundreds of results that begin with “assuming the Riemann hypothesis” become certain. Or maybe not at all. It is like being told how a story ends, with no way to read the story. We know that. We don’t know why.

2. Searching, or discovering?

The easy answer is that the machine only searches, so fast that it looks like thinking, the way a magician’s fast hands look like magic. I don’t think that answer is honest anymore. These systems now find things nobody had found (Partly Wrong, Mostly Right). That looks like discovery. Sometimes it looks like invention.

So what is a discovery, if not a search that ends somewhere new? A discovery finds what was already there; an invention makes what was not. But both begin the same way: try, fail, keep what works. In 1976, Allen Newell and Herbert Simon, two founders of the field, went further. They proposed that intelligence is search, guided by good rules of thumb (Newell and Simon 1976). If they were right, there is no line between searching and inventing. Only a difference in who pays for the search, and how.

Chess shows the difference. In 1997, Deep Blue beat the world champion by examining well over a hundred million positions every second (Campbell et al. 2002). Decades earlier, the psychologist Adriaan de Groot had asked strong players to think aloud. The grandmasters did not look at more moves than weaker players. They looked at a handful, and saw the good ones first (de Groot 1965).

Then, in 2017, AlphaZero beat the strongest chess program of the time while looking at about a thousand times fewer positions (Silver et al. 2018). It played more like the grandmaster. But it had not escaped the search. It had moved it: millions of games against itself, played before the match, in training.

So here is how I now see it. Understanding is search, paid for in advance. The grandmaster paid with years of games. AlphaZero paid in a data centre. We could never try everything, so we had to understand. The question is not whether a machine searches. It is how it paid for the search.

3. What made us

Nobody designed the human brain. Selection did, working over time: hundreds of millions of years of small changes, most of them failures, kept only when they worked. Then each of us added a childhood.

That was not cheap. Evolution ran on the sunlight of the whole living world, through billions of lives. But the cost was spread so thin, over so much time, that each step was almost free.

What it left is small. Everything we inherit at birth is written in about three billion letters of DNA (Nurk et al. 2022), less than a gigabyte by my count. Hundreds of millions of years, packed small. Then a child learns language from very little: fewer than a hundred million words heard by the age of thirteen, by researchers’ estimates (Warstadt et al. 2023). Our eyes take in far more than words, of course. But for language, that is enough.

And the brain does all this on about twenty watts, a small light bulb (Raichle and Gusnard 2002; Balasubramanian 2021). The marvel is not only that it thinks on twenty watts. It learns on twenty watts, every day of its life. Today’s models learn in a data centre, and then they stop.

The researcher François Chollet proposed measuring intelligence just this way. Not by what a system can do, but by how little experience it needs to learn it (Chollet 2019). By that measure, the child is still far ahead.

4. Borrowed time

When I say that time made us, I mean three different things. The time evolution took to design us. The time a childhood takes to fill a brain. And the slowness of thinking itself. Each time, nature spent time instead of energy.

We want the same mind without the wait. But time does not vanish when we skip it. We pay for it in another currency.

First, we borrow it. Llama 3, an open model from 2024, was trained on more than fifteen trillion tokens, the small pieces of words these models read (Meta 2024). By my own count, that is roughly a hundred thousand times what a child hears by thirteen. And every page of it was written by a person, over centuries. So we did not really skip the time. We borrowed it, from everyone who ever wrote. Data is time, stored.

Then we pay in power. The International Energy Agency estimates that data centres used about one and a half percent of the world’s electricity in 2024. It expects that to more than double by 2030, mostly because of AI (International Energy Agency 2025). I must be careful here. One trained model is shared by millions of people, so counted per answer, it can be cheap. The point is not that one answer needs a power station. It is that building these minds fast gathers, in one place, what nature spread over ages.

Physics has a name for this trade. In thermodynamics, a process run very slowly can waste almost nothing. Run it faster, and it wastes more. In 1975, Frank Curzon and Boris Ahlborn worked out what this means for an engine. Run it for the most power, and it is much less efficient than it could be. Push it faster still, and you get less power, not more (Curzon and Ahlborn 1975). Chips obey the same rule: to switch faster, they need a higher voltage, and the energy lost grows with its square (Horowitz 2014).

The brain took the other side of the trade. On average, a neuron fires only a few times a second (Attwell and Laughlin 2001); a chip switches billions of times. The brain is slow, it works in parallel, and it keeps its memory right next to its computing. That matters, because moving data costs more than computing on it. In a chip, reading a number from main memory takes more than a hundred times the energy of multiplying two numbers (Horowitz 2014).

So the trade is simple. Nature paid in time. We pay in borrowed time and power.

5. Can haste cost us time?

Now the harder question. If we could shrink that time to almost nothing, what would we really gain, as human beings? Is it as much as we believe?

Sometimes, yes. Innovation is not always bad, and physics agrees: an engine run infinitely slowly gives no power at all. There is a right speed, and it is not zero. When a disease spreads, every month saved is lives saved. A proof found in 41 days instead of twelve years frees those years for something else.

But physics also says there is a speed past which you get less. An engine is not a society, and I use it as an image, not a proof. Still, I think we can go past that speed too, and lose time in the end. Borrowed time is paid back, sometimes with interest.

Look at the proof. Found in 41 days, it may take decades to understand: the prime number theorem waited 84 years for its four-page proof. A proof found by hand comes with its understanding. A proof found by a machine leaves that work still to do. We saved the finding, and kept the whole debt of understanding.

Look at work. In 2025, researchers ran a careful experiment with sixteen experienced programmers, on real tasks in their own projects. With the AI tools of early 2025, they took 19 percent longer. Yet afterwards, they believed the tools had made them about 20 percent faster (Becker et al. 2025). It is one small study, and the tools have improved since. But it shows how a feeling of speed can hide a real delay.

Look at us. The sociologist Hartmut Rosa describes a strange loop. The tools that save us time speed up life so much that we end up with less of it (Rosa 2013). The answers arrive faster. We do not. A child still needs twenty years, and a society still needs years to live with something new.

So yes, I think we can say it, carefully. Going too fast, too far, can cost us time in the end. Not always. But when answers arrive faster than we can understand them and check them, the time we saved comes back as time we owe.

Two thousand years ago, the Roman philosopher Seneca wrote that our life is not short; we waste much of it (Seneca 2004). Maybe the problem was never that we had too little time.

6. A small brain, close to us

I am not against AI. I use it in my work every day. But I believe the AI that will matter in our lives is not the giant we rent by the word. It is a small brain, close to us: in our phone, our house, the machine on the factory floor. Good at many things, learning from us, staying with us. I sketched it in An Ecosystem, Not an Oracle and Fast AI.

The physics points the same way: keep memory next to computing, run slowly and in parallel, learn where you live. That is how the brain saves energy. The big models may be the scaffolding we need while we build. But nobody mistakes the scaffolding for the house.

7. The right speed

So why are we running? Competition, money, the fear of being second. And the real excitement of something new every week. I feel it too.

I don’t ask for time because nature was slow. Nature has no values. I ask for it because of what does not speed up: understanding, trust, and a society learning to live with what it made. Physics does not tell us to stop. It tells us that every speed has a price, and that the fastest is never the cheapest.

Time made a mind that learns on twenty watts. We are building minds on borrowed time. Borrowed time is always paid back.

I would rather pay it now, in time, than later, with interest.
